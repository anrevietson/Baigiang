# The greatness of the Son

Second Reading (Heb. 1:1-6). The first section of the epistle to the Hebrews expresses the greatness of the Son of God incarnate and particularly his superiority over the angels and the superiority of revelation in the person of Jesus Christ over all previous revelations. Unlike all the other epistles (except the first epistle of John) this letter does not begin with a greeting but with a prologue setting out the finality of Christ. In the Old Testament period revelation was fragmentary and progressive. It had occurred through deeds and words, through the history of the Israelites where they saw God at work and through prophetic preaching, The writer places us in 'the last days', that is, the eschatological Or messianic era (cf. Is 2 :2; Jer. 23 :20; Ezek. 38 :16; Dt. 10 :14). The incarnation of the Son revealed God to us in the fullest possible way here on earth. The Son is not adopted but is heir by nature to the glory of the Father. Although he became incarnate in time, the Son is eternal and he had a part in the creation of all things. In Old Testament thought, Wisdom was with God from the beginning and the author here has probably in mind the idea of the Son as the true Wisdom of God. This idea continues in v.3 where expressions are applied to Christ which were formerly applied to Wisdom (cf. Wis. 7 : 22ff; 8 :1). Jesus the Son is the perfect image and reflection of the Father. As Yahweh created all by his word, so the Son sustains all. The role of the Son incarnate is redemptive or soteriological (cf. Wis. 9 :18). Now that he has assumed our human condition and delivered us from slavery to sin, he has been raised to a position next to God himself, divine Majesty (cf. Mark 14 :62). In this enthronement of Jesus Ps. 110(109): 1 is fulfilled (cf. Acts 2 :34-36; Rom. 8 :34). In semitic thought the name was very important. Jesus has the name 'Son', a name which is higher than that of angel and the angels must worship him. The epistle says that Jesus inherited the title Son, that is, from the aspect of his human nature. As in Rom. 1:3, the writer believes that in his human nature Jesus became Son of God in the fullest sense at his resurrection but, of course, in his divine nature he is eternally Son (Rom. 8 :3; Gal. 4:4).
