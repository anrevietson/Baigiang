# The empty tomb

The Gospel (John 20:1-9). It is well known that in general all the gospels preserve two different kinds of post-resurrection story, the appearances of the risen Christ and the discovery of the empty tomb. It is also well known that these narratives stubbornly resist any attempt to be harmonized into a unified series, or to be got to correspond with the early list of appearances which Paul details at 1 Corinthians 15:5-8. However, this fact should not cause any concern. The apparent confusion in the narratives may well be related to the nature of the event in question. Whereas the passion accounts are in substantial agreement in regard to the place and time of the events narrated, presumably because they deal with external events that are easily verified, the appearances of the risen Christ are of their nature much less easily identified and located. Obviously, no attempt has been made to present the events in a unified way and this is an important indication of their essential reliability. The Christian preacher today will do well to follow the example of the evangelists in presenting the Easter message and resist any temptation to harmonize the gospel readings for this week's liturgy.
The Sunday gospel text deals with the discovery of the empty tomb by Peter and 'the beloved disciple'. An earlier version of the same incident would seem to underlie Luke 24:12 : 'But Peter rose and ran to the tomb; stooping and looking in, he saw the linen cloths by themselves; and he went home wondering at what had happened.' The fact that according to this account Peter did not grasp the meaning of what he saw would give point to a certain unevenness in John's narrative. In verse eight it is stated explicitly that 'the beloved disciple saw and believed', while in verse nine and following this recognition would appear to be contradicted :
'For as yet they did not know the scripture that he must rise from the dead. Then the disciples went back to their homes.' This would appear to correspond with the remark in Luke that Peter returned home wondering, implying that as yet there was no explicit recognition of the meaning of the empty tomb. The Jerusalem Bible translation seems to gloss over this difficulty in the Greek text by translating verse nine : 'Till this moment they had failed to understand the scriptures', implying that from now on both disciples understood fully the implication of the empty tomb. I prefer to think that the writer of the fourth gospel (the authorship of which in its present form I would not attribute directly to John the apostle but to somebody closely attached to him) had an account similar to that of Luke's in front of him and introduced an explicit reference to the 'beloved disciple' seeing and believing. This would have been easier for him since Luke 24:24 speaks of 'some of those that were with us being at the tomb', thereby recognizing that more than Peter had gone to the tomb to verify for themselves the report of Mary and the other women. The author of the fourth gospel has merely highlighted the drama of the incident by having Peter and 'the beloved disciple' go together to the tomb.
If this is the correct genesis of the account in John 20:1-9 it is clear that the main emphasis is the faith of the beloved disciple. Any implied contrast between him and Peter is secondary to the writer's main purpose. Indeed elsewhere in the fourth gospel 'the beloved disciple' (John 13:23; 19:26; 20:2; 21:7, 20) or the 'other disciple' (John 18:15 f.; 20:3, 4, 8; the two designations are identified at 20:2) and Peter are shown to be perfectly harmonious (13:23-25; 18:15-16; 21:7) and the primacy of Peter is recognized and freely accepted (6:68 f.; 21:15-17). Yet there can be no denying the special relationship of the beloved disciple to Jesus in the eyes of the fourth evangelist: he leans on Jesus' breast during the Last Supper (13:23-26); he stands beside the cross and is entrusted with the care of his mother by Jesus (19:25-27); he outruns Peter in his enthusiasm to get to the tomb (20:2-9); he recognizes Jesus on the shore as he is fishing with Peter (21:7); his death is obviously causing concern within the community and a word of Jesus explaining it is given (21:24); the testimony of the beloved disciple is the source of the things narrated in the gospel (21:24). Thus, in handling this text on Easter morning the preacher will do well to contrast the different ways to faith of Peter (appearance of the risen Lord in the first reading) and John (discovery of the empty tomb in the third reading).
The designation of John as the disciple Jesus loved indicates the nature of his relationship with Jesus; it is one based on mutual love and trust, an idea that is central to the whole Johannine proclamation. God is love (1 John 4:8, 16) and his love is shown in the giving of his Son for the world (John 3:16; 1 John 4:9). Jesus himself shares in this restless, self-sacrificing love of the Father :
'Having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end' (13:1), an end that was death itself (15:13). His true disciples are those who abide in his love (15:10), by loving one another as he has loved them (15:12 f.). In other words love is the hallmark of the true disciple, and the beloved disciple is beloved precisely because he has responded to that call. For the author of the fourth gospel the role of love in arriving at true faith is of primary importance, a lesson he has learned from the beloved disciple. Genuine love leads to faith because it enables one to appreciate the other for his true worth. It excludes all self-seeking and so permits one to evaluate the other's true dignity properly. Normally we think of faith as leading to love or expressing itself in love, 'faith active in love' (Gal. 5:6), but for the fourth evangelist the order is reversed once the true nature of love is understood on the basis of Jesus' life of love. The beloved disciple's faith in the risen Christ is not just based on the discovery of the empty tomb therefore. Rather it is the outgrowth of his loving personal relationship with Jesus, and his recognition from the presence of the burial cloths that Jesus had given his all in love.
The beloved disciple 'saw and believed'. A little later the risen Christ will commend to Thomas those 'who have not seen and have believed' (John 20:29). This does not mean that the faith of the beloved disciple is in any way inferior, just because he has seen. For the Johannine Church the unique role of the official witnesses is of special importance since the whole faith of the Church was based on their seeing: John 19:25; 20:30 f.; 1 John 1:1-4. This seeing' was something more than mere external observation of the events of Jesus' life. It involved penetrating the mystery of his life and person, recognizing the genuine signs of his glory which lead to faith in him. Of course it was the recognition of the great sign of the cross as the act of love par excellence that made all the other signs intelligible. Love not merely disposes a man to recognize the true dignity of Jesus; it is when Jesus is recognized as love that his true dignity is revealed. This is the Easter message for the Johannine Church from the beloved disciple. It corresponds with and complements perfectly Peter's message of peace and reconciliation to the house of Cornelius, both of vital interest for all the Christian people on every subsequent Easter morning.
