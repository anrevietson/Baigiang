# The opening of Acts

First Reading (Acts 1:1-11). These opening verses of the Acts of the Apostles present the book to us as the author's second volume. The earlier work to which they refer is the Third Gospel, written by Luke who also gave us the Acts. In the closing verses of the Third Gospel the risen saviour, before his ascension, tells his disciples that they are witnesses of his saving work, chosen by God to preach repentance for the forgiveness of sins in his name to all nations, beginning with Jerusalem. But before they go forth to preach, the Holy Spirit must first descend on them in the holy city. The power of the apostolic preaching is the Holy Spirit whose action is one of the most marked features of the story of the early Church as recounted in the Acts of the Apostles. .
At the end of Luke's Gospel we find the apostles in Jerusalem joyfully awaiting the coming of the Spirit. The emphasis of the first verses of Acts is subtly different, pointed by the interpretation which the angels give. Jesus will no longer be present with them by a bodily presence, but his place as their guide and strength will be taken by the Spirit. The emphasis is all forward looking and workmanlike : they are not to waste their time and energies in wondering idly when the end will come, but are to get on with their mission of bearing witness. The programme is given them : as the climax of Luke's Gospel was at Jerusalem - and Luke had built up to this carefully by his extended narrative of the journey up to Jerusalem and the passion, during which he had placed a major part of Jesus' teaching - so now the message is to spread from Jerusalem. Jerusalem is the pivot and from there the Acts will show Christ's message spreading in ever-widening circles, first to Judaea and Samaria and finally to Rome, called in some contemporary Jewish documents 'the end of the earth', though it might also be described as the centre of the earth because of its political importance a~ capital of the empire.
It is difficult to imagine what exactly the phenomena of the ascension must have consisted in, exactly how Jesus' bodily presence on earth was terminated, for the elements of the description are drawn from stock biblical images, so that they show as much the meaning as the event of the ascension; The cloud is always a symbol of the divine, so that it means that Jesus is drawn up into the divine sphere; and in this case it is surely connected with the promise that Jesus will come again 'on a cloud with power and great glory' (Luke 21:27) when the powers of heaven are shaken, etc. The description is perhaps modelled upon the assumption of Elijah (2 Kings 2) when his spirit came upon his disciple Elisha, as the Spirit of Jesus is to come upon the apostles. And the angels who appear to give an interpretation of the event have many forebears in the Bible as vehicles of the inspired interpretation of an event. Especially throughout the Acts, where Luke is moulding his material with some freedom, he is accustomed to teach theology by means of such little pictorial scenes.
