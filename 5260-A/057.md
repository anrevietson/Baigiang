# Conversion is ongoing

First Reading (Ezek. 18:25-28). No one is established once for all in virtue or in faith; these are not static conditions. We are all weak, capable of change whether for good or ill. But God sees our heart.
The idea was common among the Jews, all through their history, that children suffered for the sins of their parents. We find it even in the time of our Lord: 'Master, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?' (John 9:1-2). One of the main occupations of the Jews in exile (to whom Ezekiel was speaking) was blaming others for what had gone wrong (see e.g. Ezek. 14:12-23). Ezekiel pointed Out to them that we cannot hide behind the goodness of others; every one of us stands before God with his own goodness, and his own badness, and is judged accordingly. Certainly the goodness or badness of each one can influence others, but the point Ezekiel wanted to make was that God judges us by what we are, not by what others are like.
Personal responsibility was one of his big themes. Ezekiel did not invent the principle of personal responsibility; this appears in the earliest traditions and laws of Israel. But he restated it in a form and with an emphasis which his own time demanded. With the principle goes a declaration of the vital importance of interior regeneration (see Ezek. 36:26 ff.).
It is wrong to blame the past for everything. Let each man look into his own heart and he will see plenty to blame. But repentance will win him pardon and life.
The Responsorial psalm is a model 'act of contrition'. It does not try to stir up an emotional sorrow: it simply states the case, admits the guilt, and asks for mercy. What more can we do before God?
