# The sacrifice of Isaac

First Reading (Gen 22:1-2, 9-13, 15-18). The biblical narrative of Abraham's sacrifice of his son Isaac (Gen 22:1-19) is a masterpiece from many points of view: literature, theology, drama, human and divine, etc.). It stands out conveying a message and inviting the reflections of readers and listeners. Nothing in the narrative is allowed to take from the central figures and events: Abraham, his only son Isaac, God's command to Abraham to sacrifice Isaac and Abraham's readiness to comply with this, a command which seemed so contradictory since God himself had told Abraham that it was through Isaac that posterity and the promises would be assured. The passage ends by noting that after his heroic obedience the promise to Abraham was renewed.
The narrative, as I said, calls for reflection and response, and this it has received down the centuries. Jewish tradition noted the mention of the third day, and regarded such mention in the biblical text as referring to salvation. The same tradition identified the mount on which Isaac was to be sacrificed (Moriah) with the Temple mount in Jerusalem and highlighted the sacrifice aspect of the account. The name Moriah is understood in v.14 as 'The Lord shall see'. Jewish tradition said he would see the sacrificial blood of Isaac. In our own time Soren Kierkegaard (Fear and Trembling, 1843) has written a notable work on the passage, introduced by a beautiful panegyric on Abraham. Among many quotable texts on Abraham he says: 'There was one who was great by reason of his power, and one who was great by reason of his wisdom, and one who was great by reason of his hope; but Abraham was greater than all, great by reason of his wisdom whose secret is foolishness, great by reason of his hope whose form is madness, great by reason of his love which is hatred of oneself'.
In whatever age one reflects on this narrative two questions stand out calling for an answer: (1) why did God ask Abraham to sacrifice his son Isaac: what kind of God could do this? Was it all divine madness?; (2) Why was Abraham (and in later Jewish tradition Isaac) so ready to comply? Attempts have been made at a solution, but without notable success.
Second Reading Tom 8:312-34). The first reading left us with the unanswered, and probably unanswerable, questions: Why did God so command Abraham? Why did Abraham obey? The present reading from Romans raises similar questions with regard to God the Father and the sacrifice of Christ. Why did God so act, in sacrificing ('not sparing') his Son? The reply, if reply there be, could be that God entered our human history with the passion of love. The sacrifice of Christ by the Father stands out as the action of Abraham does, only considerably more so. We may not be able to answer why. What God wants us to understand is that this his action shows that he is on our side; that he has freely and out of passionate love let us know that his concern is deep and sincere.
