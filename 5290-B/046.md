# Our great high priest

Second Reading (Heb. 4 :14-16). The congregation addressed in the epistle to the Hebrews is sluggish and discouraged. The author is particularly anxious to banish any trace of despair or hopelessness that would arise from the experience of weakness. In order to encourage his readers he highlights the doctrine of the priesthood of the risen Christ. Jesus is one who has passed securely beyond the veil and now stands ready to assist us, an anchor of our lives as sure as it is firm (cf. Heb. 6 :19). It is a priesthood that comes with the resurrection; it is of 'the order of Melchizedek and forever'. The emphasis here is on the foreverness of this priesthood made possible through the resurrection. The author pinpoints exactly what he means by this priesthood in Heb. 7 :16. Christ is a priest 'by the power of indestructible life'. In consequence we are left with the conviction of his readiness to help 'his p9wer to save is utterly certain, since he is living forever to intercede' (7 :25). The priesthood of the epistle to the Hebrews is not immediately, therefore, a priesthood of crucifixion and death, but an activity of the exalted living Lord.
Although exalted and beyond life's cares, this priest is not without sympathy and compassion. He himself has tasted to the full the weaknesses, temptations and general fragility of human life. Our priest is one who has shared our life with us and therefore knows it from within. There is much in the New Testament about the revealing role of Christ. He is God focused down to human size and displayed in human language and character. We too are revealed to God in Christ when he experiences from within what it is to live in the limitations of human flesh (cf. Heb. 5 :2). Christ's priesthood according to this epistle can be reduced to a permanence of intercession, understanding, tolerance, sympathy and love.
