# Christ exalted

Second Reading (Eph. 1:17-23). More openly theological is the second reading from Ephesians. Here the ascension is viewed as the logical conclusion and completion of the resurrection, without of course any interest being shown in a period of time separating the one phase of the movement from the other. (It is only the Acts, of all the New Testament, that mentions this gap, hut there it has the clear function of a period of preparation.) The passage must be viewed against the background of the epistle as a whole. The letter appears to be sprung from a controversy over the position of Christ in the universe. There were some who claimed that he should be classed among a host of beings, intermediaries between God and man, somewhat similar to angels in the Hebrew tradition but called in the Greek tradition by more abstract names : Sovereign-ties, Authorities, Powers, Dominations (Gabriel means the Power of God, but these beings originate in pagan Greek philosophical tradition). It seems that it was this view which finally led Paul to formulate his own in opposition. The meaning of the resurrection-ascension event is that God has raised Christ above these nebulous powers to his due position as crown of creation ; first-born of all creation by his nature, and first to be born from the dead by his resurrection, he is first in every way (Col. 1:15-18).
But what interests Paul at the moment is the consequences of this for Christians. The Father's exaltation of Christ to this position is evidence of 'the power that he has exercised for us believers' . The link in this chain of argument is that Christ is 'the head of the Church', that is that in Christ are contained all Christians, who constitute his body. This is one of Paul's fundamental insights, dating from the vision of Christ on the road to Damascus when he learnt that in persecuting his members he was persecuting Christ himself ; it permeates his whole theology. If Christians are baptized into Christ, they have risen with him and with him have been exalted to heaven, and with him glorified (Rom. 8:30). In another sense the transformation into the glorious Christ is still to come ; at least it has yet to be revealed (Col. 3:4), and it is presumably in this sense that Paul can write elsewhere that the Father 'will raise us with Jesus in our turn' (2 Cor. 4:13). But the basis of the Christian hope is the ascension in that by it the Christ in whom we are already contained is raised to the right hand of the Father and the power thus shown is at work in the Church.
