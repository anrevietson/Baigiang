# Jesus appears to the disciples

The Gospel (John 20:19-31). Since chapter twenty-one is an appendix to St John's Gospel the passage we read today is the conclusion of the Gospel proper. St Thomas's profession of faith is a fitting climax to the Gospel that was written 'so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing this you may have life through his name' (v.31).
Our reading contains two apparition stories which show that Jesus whom the disciples had known had returned from the tomb although his body was now glorified and spiritualized. The disciples who saw the hands and side of the risen one and Thomas who was invited to touch his wounds had clear evidence that the Jesus who had been crucified had risen. On the other hand his ability to pass through closed doors was a proof that he was no longer subject to the conditions of ordinary mortals. The risen Jesus greeted his disciples with the common Jewish salutation peace be with you'. The Jews considered peace a gift that comes from God, something which established a relation of harmony and friendship not only between the Israelite and his neighbour but also between the Israelite and his God. Jesus had declared during h~5 lifetime that the world cannot give true peace and that he had come to bestow it (John 14:27). Now as glorified Lord he communicates his peace to his disciples and thus binds them to himself and to one another in a union of love and harmony. The disciples rejoice at seeing their beloved master again and they are made to realise that the sad events that surrounded his death were only a prelude to a deep spiritual joy (cf. John 16:22). In Jewish circles it was thought that the messianic age would be characterised by peace and joy. Therefore when John says that Jesus conferred true peace and joy on his followers he would seem to be suggesting that the messianic age had come.
Earlier in the Gospel (17:17-19) John had told us that Jesus had prayed for the disciples whom he was to send into the world. Now he tells us that the risen Jesus, before leaving this world, gave to the disciples the task of continuing his mission and of communicating to all men the salvation he had achieved (cf. Mat. 28:19). But the disciples needed to be purified and strengthened for such a great task. Therefore Jesus sent his Spirit on them to sanctify them so that they in turn could sanctify others. It was the privilege of Jesus who was already glorified by the Father to communicate the Spirit of life to his followers (cf. John 7:39).
One of the effects of the gift of the Spirit on the disciples was that they could forgive sins. John does not tell us who could exercise this power or how it should be exercised. The Catholic Church uses this text to support its doctrine and practice of the sacrament of Penance. It would, however, be precarious to argue that this text alone gives a sufficient basis for the Church's teaching in this matter. Yet one can say with confidence that the Church's use of the sacrament of Penance is one valid and legitimate way of exercising the power over sin given by the risen Jesus to his disciples.
The story of doubting Thomas shows that the disciples who saw the risen Lord had no real advantage over later believers. Thomas refused to accept the word of the other disciples who had seen the Lord and insisted that he needed visible and tangible proof of the resurrection. Jesus gently upbraided him and gave him to understand that there is no need for such a proof and that one can reasonably believe on the evidence of others. Thomas represents all of us who must believe without seeing. But our faith is in no way inferior to that of the disciples who had the joy of actually seeing the risen one. Like them we can rejoice in the assurance that Jesus has risen. We can enjoy the peace which he bestows on all believers. We can open ourselves to the gift of his Spirit whom he gives to us and like true disciples we can strive to continue the mission of Christ and bring salvation to others.
