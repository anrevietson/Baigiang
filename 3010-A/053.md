# The temptations of Jesus

The Gospel (Mat. 4:1-11). The Gospel reading today completes the message of the extracts from Genesis and Romans. In the first two readings we were invited to face up to the evil of sin, and to renew our faith and confidence in the remedy provided by God : final victory for the human race under the leadership of God's Son. The Gospel reading describes graphically the first great encounter between Christ and the forces of evil, between our new leader and protagonist and the prince of evil.
Matthew, like Luke, has teased out Mark's summary account of the temptation. Did the two later evangelists have other records to draw upon - memories of Christ's confidants who had been entrusted with fuller details of the visionary temptations? Or are we sharing here the insights that a fervent group of Jewish converts drew from prayerful reflection? Have the details been filled out by meditation on the kind of testing that Christ had to face all during his public ministry (John 6:25-27; 7:3, 4; 6:15; cf. Heb. 2:18; 4:15; Luke 2:19, 51)? There certainly seems to be a careful linking up of the Old Testament sources of promise with their fulfilment in Christ. God's Son, founder of God's new people, wanders for a time in desert place like Israel of old; but unlike her, he withstands all attempts to shake him from his purpose. Like Moses and Elijah he fasts for forty days and communes with God; like them he stands alone on a mountain, ushering in a new era of intimate familiarity with God. Whatever the literary background of Matthew's description, the essential message remains the same Confronted like the first Adam with Satan's stratagems, Christ at once pinpoints the flaws and routs his enemy in ignominious defeat.
It wasn't easy for the devil to tempt Christ. In all temptation there must be a half-truth, an appearance of good. But in tempting Christ, Satan realises that this time he must aim at making the picture appear all good. And this is what he tries to do. When a man is famished after a long fast in God's honour, there is no harm in satisfying his bodily needs with food. And hasn't Christ come to establish God's kingdom on earth? Wouldn't it help if he could gather the people round him by a spectacular display of power? And wouldn't the work be accomplished much more quickly and painlessly, if he could be crowned King of Israel right from the start? The main flaw in all these suggestions is that the Father has planned it otherwise. The tree may still be a delight to the eyes; but its fruit is forbidden. Unhesitatingly, Christ unmasks the pretexts, matching scripture text for text. Humble dependence on the Father's care; no cheap ready-made publicity; no speedy, painless achievement of the end in view - this is the way marked out by the Father.
Twenty centuries after Christ's encounter with Satan in the Judean desert, the temptation to do good 'in our own way' is still very much with Us. The impatient use of violence when things aren't working out as we planned; unduly pressurising others to make them see things as we do, instead of trying to win them to our point of view; resisting or side-stepping the clear directions of legitimate authority within the sphere of its competence; in fact, the tempting short-cuts to do good - regardless of the possible harmful consequences. The mills of God grind slowly - for us as they did for Christ. Everything we undertake during our brief pilgrim passage to the Father remains necessarily incomplete and imperfect - awaiting the finishing touches of Christ when he comes in glory. On that day, whatever the failures and frustrations 0£ our best efforts to do good, everything will become part of Christ's triumph and ours - provided it is genuine coin bearing the stamp of the Father's will.
