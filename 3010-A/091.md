# The fall

First Reading (Gen. 2:7-9, 3:1-7). During Lent the Church invites us to examine our lives, to repent of our sins and to do penance for them. Very appropriately, the first lesson today introduces this Lenten theme by bringing us face to face with the origins of Sin. The sacred writer has culled his narrative from one of the ancient story-tellers in Israel; and his account presents us with a colourful picture, full of eastern symbolism and echoing tales of times long past.
Having moulded man from the slime of the earth, God plants a garden in Eden - a place which in eastern lands especially would have seem 'out of this world' of slime and toil. The man and his wife walk God's garden, sharing a happiness that surpasses anything they could have hoped for, enjoying intimate familiarity with God.
In subtle form, temptation makes its presence felt. It has all been carefully planned by the enemy of God's designs and the serpent form assumed by the tempter may well reflect Israel's contempt for pagan worship of the time - for the serpent that received divine honours and that was, at the same time, typical of debased and immoral Canaanite temple worship. Graphically the story-teller fills in the psychological stages in temptation : the half-truth 'the tree was good for food and . . . a delight to the eyes'; the whole lie 'you will be like God'; the fatal dallying with evil's attraction; and the final yielding to sin, with had example and specious persuasion immediately spreading the contagion.
At once and within themselves, the man and the woman experience the destructive effects of sin : the sense of shame and alienation from God; the painful discovery of their innate poverty and nakedness - all that a creature can claim as his very own; the tardy realisation that man's happiness is inseparably interwoven with God's plans for him. In the Responsorial Psalm (Ps. 50) we confess that we are all sinners and call on God to forgive us.
