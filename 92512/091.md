# The census of Quirinius

Gospel (Lk 2:1-14). Various historical or technical difficulties arise with regard to vv. 1-3, the details of which lie outside the scope of this commentary. Suffice it to say that Quirinius did not become govenor in Syria until AD 6 and it was after that that he conducted a census in Judea that provoked the rebellion of Judas the Galilean (Acts 5:37). If Jesus birth took place during Herod's reign it cannot have taken place during the census several years later. Many solutions have been suggested - none totally convincing. It may be that Luke places the birth of Jesus in a loose historical setting, mentioning events that took place around the time and certainly these events in a remote Judean territory would not have been of particular interest to the Greeks of the Empire seventy or eighty years later.
However, by mentioning Caesar Augustus, Luke makes a delicate contrast. For the Roman Empire of the year 70, Caesar Augustus was seen as the inaugurator of peace and the great world benefactor. On the other hand, Jesus is presented in the poverty and helplessness of babyhood as the bringer of peace to all: it is Jesus, not Augustus, who is the Saviour of the world. Luke here presents Jesus as of the Davidic line and mention of Bethlehem is a harking back to David and Soloman. 'Manger' appears as a potent word for Luke symbolising Jesus as the sustenance for the world. It is noteworthy how often, throughout the Gospel, Luke refers to eating and drinking as a symbol for close friend-ship and union with God. Mention of the Shepherds may be an echo of David's humble origins though more in accord with Luke's theme of poverty. 'Mangy, stinking, bathless shepherds are in their ritual uncleanness an encouragement for all who lack religious status'.
