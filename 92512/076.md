# The birth of Jesus

The Gospel (Luke 2 :1-14). The Gospel of the Dawn Mass will take up where this reading ends. In his Infancy Narrative Luke, in common with Matthew, speaks of the virgin birth and of Bethlehem as the place where Jesus was born. But Luke views the events more from Mary's point of view, while Matthew in general sets out to show Jesus' legal descent from David through Joseph. According to the prophecy of Micab (Mic. 5 :2-5), a prince of ancient lineage is to be born in Bethlehem and he will be the shepherd of the scattered flock of Israel; he will stand in the glory of the Lord and extend his dominion to the ends of the earth with a proclamation of peace. Luke must have had this prophecy in mind when writing his narrative. In keeping with his intention (1:1-4) Luke situates in history the birth of Christ. Augustus was emperor 30 BC - 14 AD. The general census of the whole world (i.e. the Roman empire) was a means of tax-assessment. According to Josephus (the Jewish historian) Quinnills carried out a Census in AD 6-7. There is a problem about the census mentioned by Luke which has not yet been solved fully, but inscriptions let us know that Quirinius had a special commission to carry out a census in Palestine in 10-8 BC. Bethlehem was the birth-place of David (1 Sam. 16 :1). It is approximately ninety miles from Nazareth. We do not know if the child was born soon after the arrival of Mary and Joseph in Bethlehem but it seems likely. The manger may have been in the inner part of a cave which was built up to form a house and the front portion of which was used as an inn for travellers. Since this front room would have been crowded with visitors (in Bethlehem for the census) Mary and Joseph would have chosen the quieter and more private atmosphere of the rear part, usually used for animals. 'Her first-born' in no way implies that Mary had other children; rather what is important is that the first-born son (who might be an only son) had certain rights. We see this from a number of places in the Old Testament. The first-born will continue the name and receive the double share of property of the ancestral line (cf. Gen. 27-29; Dt. 21 :17). Moreover there was a law prescribing the consecration of the first-born male to God (Exod. 13 :2, 12; 34 :19). It is probably the strong Semitic character of 1-2 that caused Luke to use prototokos (first-born) instead of monogenes (only) (cf. 7 :12). The mention of the shepherds recalls David who was a shepherd (cf. 1 Sam. 17 34ff.). Here, as throughout his gospel, Luke gives a special place to the poor and humble. They are the first to receive the good news. The 'glory of the Lord' is an expression frequent in the Old Testament (Exod. 13 :21; 16 :10) and is generally associated with the great redeeming acts of God, and with the Ark or Temple (cf. Exod. 14 :4, 18; 40 :34ff). Luke generally reserves the term 'glory' to the Ascension or parousia but again the Semitic characterof these chapters brings it in here. Here, as often, Luke emphasises the presence of the day of salvation - 'today', the long-.awaited day of Israel's salvation has dawned. The Old Testament promises are fulfilled (eight times in chs. 1-2 Luke refers to fulfilment). The song of the angels gives a liturgical setting to the birth of Jesus as well as an eschatological note.
