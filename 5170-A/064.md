# God works for good

Second Reading (Rom. 8 :28-30). Today's reading from Romans is a difficult one, but perhaps it is we who have made it so by reading into it what Paul never intended, a theology of predestination. The leading idea in the passage is not our notion of predestination but the idea that our ultimate security lies in God and his 'purpose'.
This security, this certainty of Paul, explains the very emphatic statements that we find scattered throughout his Epistles: 'but we know . . ' (Rom. 8:28; 13 :11; 'I reckon . . ' (Rom. 8:18); 'for I am sure . . ' (Rom. 8:38); 'For this perishable nature must put on the imperishable' (1 Cor. 15 :53).
The 'purpose' of God is described in verse twenty-nine. It is that we bear the same image as hi5 Son, 'so that he may be the eldest in a large company of brothers'. The brotherhood in Christ of those 'whom God foreordained' is a necessary corollary to our adaptation as sons, which in Gelatines is declared to be God's purpose in sending his Son (Gal. 4 :4-5). Later in Romans it is stated that this brotherhood will include all mankind (11:32). In Christ the barriers dividing mankind are broken down (cf. Gal. 3 :28).
Is Paul here simply dreaming the impossible dream of Christian, let alone human, brotherhood? Paul would answer with a very definite 'no'. Because in God's purpose he has an unshakeable belief. It was 'God who was in Christ reconciling the world to himself' (2 Cor. 5 :19), the God who raised Jesus from the dead. The apostle's confidence is so sure, God's purpose so certain, that he can end the passage by describing a future event in the past tense, as though it were already realised: 'those whom he justified he also glorified' . However difficult and even diverse are the statements in Paul's letters about the future, 'they reveal a fixed basic ' motif in his theology viewed as a whole: the sovereignty and final victory of God, who alone is to bring the work he began to completion' (G. Cornkamm, Paid, p.22S).
