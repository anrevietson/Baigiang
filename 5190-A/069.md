# Paul's anguish

When Paul announced his intention in the letter's early verses, he leaves his flank uncovered. He had said: the Gospel is the power of God saving all who have faith - Jews first, but Greeks as well (1:16). Salvation comes from the Jews. Yet the Jews receive a write-up which is far from flattering, and verges on the feeling that the are worse than the pagans because they should have known better. How then are the Jews saved first. They do not seem to have been saved at all.
Paul is consistent enough in his pastoral mission and his theology - he bows to the tradition that Jews must be offered the Gospel first, as the Lord himself did. However, the doctrinal exposition of this letter is concerned with more fundamental issues than the division Jew/Gentile. For, in the last resort, a man's friendship with God does not depend on being one or the other. The previous chapters of the letter have thus concentrated on the fact of salvation by faith, and the qualities of that salvation, leading to Paul's confession to the gift of Christ's Spirit as the achievement of our reconciliation with the Father.
Now God's revelation did not come out of the blue. Paul is far from ashamed of his Jewish pedigree and education. He never calls his Christian life a conversion from Judaism; rather he was called by God, called by God's Son on the road to Damascus, as Israel was called by this same Christ. God called Israel to faith from the time of the man of faith, the patriarch Abraham, and for Paul this was an anticipated call of Israel to faith in the rock to come. Thus they were invited first. Their devotion was invited through the many gifts and promises they received, out of God's love but also in anticipation of the day when from their flesh and blood' would come 'Christ who is above all'. The apostle is concerned then with the offering of salvation, the invitation to faith. But he does leave himself open to criticism that Judaism is irrelevant now the Gentiles have an equal chance at response, yes, even a better one uncluttered as they are by dreams of the past.
In today's reading Paul begins his answer to what must have been actual criticism of his mission to the Gentiles. He does face facts. Jesus was born of Israel; Paul was born of Israel; Israel was really called by God, actually received the promises. Things went Wrong because they did not and do not accept their vocation. Paul himself did accept and is thus desperately anxious that his people should do the Same; he is even willing to give up his most precious gift for their sakes - his own communion with Christ (obviously being hyperbolic here). How Israel could miss their opportunities and how they will regain them is the subject of the next three chapters of Romans (9-11).
This letter obliquely fortifies a central message of today's Mass, the crucial importance of faith : faith as the antidote to discouragement; faith as the confession of God'5 plan for his creation; faith in Christ as the only source of full union with God and one another; faith as the only hope that God is in charge of everything we know.
