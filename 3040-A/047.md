# Children of light

Second Reading (Eph. 5:8-14). St Paul here uses the contrast between light and darkness to express the contrast which exists between human nature without Christ (darkness) and human nature which has received the impression of God's Holy Spirit with a view to the day of redemption (light); cf. Ephesians 4:30.
Our passage begins with the statement : 'You are now light in Christ.' This is not a phrase which is easy to translate into meaningful English but its general sense is fairly clear; and it is made clearer by the phrase which follows : 'You must walk as becomes the children of light.' According to Aramaic usage 'children' affirms a vague association. Here it indicates those who have been illuminated and penetrated by this metaphorical light, which is God's Spirit or grace. The term 'to walk' is a quite frequent metaphor to designate a general style of life. St Paul, therefore, exhorts his readers to adopt a life style which is appropriate to those who have received God's light.
Here, as frequently elsewhere, St Paul's moral exhortation is very earnest but quite general. He does not go into specific details as to what is right or wrong (light or darkness) in any given situation.
St Paul then introduces an additional metaphor taken from farming or gardening : they must produce a crop of actions such as one would expect from 'children of the light' who are walking as they should. This is also a frequent Semitic metaphor which occurs in many pages of the Gospels : from the place in which St John the Baptist urges his converts to 'bring forth fruits worthy of repentance' (Luke 3:8), through parables in which our Lord compares the word of God to a seed (e.g. Luke 8:11), to our Lord's final words to his disciples (John 15:16).
St Paul summarises the 'fruits of the light' as goodness, justice and truth. One can understand goodness as the virtue by which a man is true to himself, always acting in a way which human nature seems to demand of us. Justice is the virtue by which we treat our fellow-men exactly as they should be treated. Truth seems to indicate complete honesty and sincerity in all our decisions. It is obvious that this list is incomplete because it does not include 'love' which is in a sense the very centre of St Paul's moral teaching and which receives full treatment in 1 Corinthians 15.
This text from Ephesians should be compared with the fuller text from Galatians 5 : 16 ff. in which St Paul gives a list of twelve 'fruits of the Spirit', starting with love and then listing eleven other typical Christian virtues. In all such texts St Paul is laying down a general pattern of Christian life, leaving us to apply these principles to actual situations with complete and utter integrity.
