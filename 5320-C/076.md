# The martyrdom of the seven brothers

First Reading (2 Macc. 7 :1-2, 9-14). This is one of the most moving stories of martyrdom in the Old Testament. It tells of a mother who preferred to watch her seven sons go to a brutal death rather than yield to the demands of Antiochus Epiphanes that they would publicly renounce their religion. For this is what was at stake. It may strike us as bizarre that somebody would suffer this sort of death rather than eat the flesh of a pig. But for the Hebrew, to eat pork was equivalent to a public rejection of the law of God, according to which the devout Jew ordered his life. It was better, then, to die nobly, rather than live a life based on the renouncement of God. But the death the sons died was not to mark the end of life for them: 'God is watching . . . "he will certainly take pity on his servants"' (v.6; Deut. 32 :36); '"inhuman fiend, you may discharge us from this present life, but the king of the world will raise us up, since it is for his laws that we die, to live again forever" ' (v.9). The third son lived in the hope of a bodily resurrection, when his limbs would be restored to him (v. 11). The persecutor, on the other hand, will not receive the new life (v.l4), but the Lord will torment him and his race (v.17). The martyrs are convinced that their sufferings have been brought on by their own sins (v. 18 f.), but they trust that their deaths may move the Lord to rescue his people from their sufferings (v.37 f.). This is the first clear statement of the resurrection of the body in the Old Testament: it is a resurrection of the just (and only the just) to an eternal life (v.9), about which St John was to write so much, and which is also discussed in the Gospel reading today.
