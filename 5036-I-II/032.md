# The stilling of the storm

The Gospel (Mark 4:35-41). The account of the stilling of the storm is one where the religious significance and practical application are close enough to the surface and must dominate our understanding of the text. There is no point in trying to reconstruct in graphic detail the actual sequence of events; what could be more helpful, however, would be an effort to tune in on the wave-length of the first hearers of the Gospel, so as to be able to pick up significant items of symbolism and background presuppositions through which the meaning came to them.
The first reading and its responsorial psalm have already indicated the range of associations conjured up for the Jewish mind by the mention of a sea-storm. So the purpose of this Gospel passage which describes Jesus taming the unruly waves is not just to fill us with wonder at a marvellous happening but to reveal a special presence of the creator's power in Jesus. Another detail is the mention of Jesus sleeping; this is not merely a touching piece of realistic description but carries its own weight of religious symbolism. The first hearers of the Gospel, steeped as they were in the Old Testament writings, would have instantly remembered that sound sleep is a sign that a man has entrusted his life totally to God; as Psalm 4 :8 puts it:
In peace I lie down and fall asleep at once,
since you alone, Yahweh, make me rest secure.
So this detail shows Jesus as living that loving abandonment to the Father's care which is a major part of his own message (see the Sermon on the Mount).
The centre of the story is found in the two words of Jesus - his commanding word to the storm and his reproachful word to the disciples. This reproach can be understood in two ways: (1) the disciples' fright could be that caused by the physical danger of the storm and their lack of faith would then mean their lack of trust in God's watchful care over them; (2) the fear could, however, also refer to their reaction to Jesus, and the faith which is missing is faith in Jesus at least as one in whom God's power is at work in a special way. The evangelist could well have intended the ambiguity in order to widen the application of the reproach.
In any case, the final phrases of the story tell of the disciples' reaction to Jesus. Filled with awe, they have a dim but unmistakable sense that some greater-than-human power is at work in their midst. Their question 'Who is this that even the wind and sea obey him?' includes to some extent its own answer. It challenges its hearers, now just as in the first century.
What then has this Gospel to say to a Christian community today? Believers today no less than the early Christians can easily see their own situation reflected in that of the disciples. As we hear of the boat tossed dangerously about on the stormy waves, we can readily think of the Church in the stresses and tensions of its existence in the world. The storms today may be caused rather by the uncertainties involved in trying to live a genuine incarnated Christian faith in the midst of our world which is in a process of rapid cultural change, but the message is the same. The followers of Jesus would be wrong to lose heart and to feel that their Lord had no interest in them, in spite of his apparent silence at times. Such an attitude would call upon itself again the reproach of Jesus: 'Why are you afraid? Have you no faith?'
The identity-question at the close of the story can set us thinking too. The disciples' previous understanding of Jesus was challenged by what happened; the same holds for the Christian community. We too are faced with the question: 'Who then is this?'; and that question has an unspoken but inescapable follow-up:
'What are we to do about it?'
