# Faith that conquers the world

Second Reading (1 John 5 :1-7). It is commonly held that the epistles of John were written by the author of the Gospel. However, in spite of the similarities, there are also differences; it might be better to speak of a Johannine school The first verse has its parallels in the Fourth Gospel (birth by faith, 1:13; birth by baptism, 3 :5). So too has the second, on loving God's children and keeping his commandments. This verse, however, is awkwardly expressed; one would expect to find it the other way round. In speaking of the 'commandment', whether in the singular or the plural, John really has only one commandment in mind - that of love. In the Gospel he calls it 'a new commandment'. This is John's substitute for 'a new covenant'. We meet it in the context of the farewell discourse, which is the last will and testament of Jesus. Here John goes on to say that 'keeping his commandments is not difficult' because faith gives victory over the world (cf. John 16 :33, 'Have confidence, I have overcome the world'). Verse 6 specifies what this faith is: that 'Jesus is the Son of God'. Then, in a polemic against deceit gnosticism, he emphasises that he is speaking of the Jesus who 'came in water and in blood', i.e. the historic Jesus who was baptised and died on the cross (not a heavenly Christ who came down upon him and left him again). 'And the Spirit is the witness.' One might be tempted to think of the Spirit's witness given to the Baptist when Jesus was baptised (John 1:33). But this does not fit the context of the epistle, where we find reference to an 'anointing' of the Spirit, an inner vision and understanding given by the Spirit. This is confirmed by the second part of the verse: 'The Spirit is the truth' (n6te the article). The 'truth' is the revelation of the divine reality. The witness of the Spirit is therefore the revelation of the Spirit within the community.
Strangely, the lectionary reading omits to give the important concluding verse: 'for there are three witnesses: the spirit and the water and the blood, and the -three are in agreement', which implies that the evidence of history and the witness of the Spirit are at one. (It is at this point that the famous comma Johanneum is introduced in the later textual tradition and the Vulgate. This is now admitted to be not genuine. If the comma were to be admitted, the motif of the passage would be changed; in the comma the water and the blood are no longer the historical baptism and death of Jesus but the sacramental events of Baptism and the Eucharist.)
