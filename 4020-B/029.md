# The mandate to forgive

The Gospel (John 20 :19-31). This passage contains John's version of our Lord's mandate to his disciples. There are parallels in the concluding verses of both Matthew and Luke. John is particularly close to Luke (see' on the Gospel for the following Sunday). The reading falls into two parts: the giving of the Spirit and the mandate (on Easter Sunday) and the confession of Thomas a week later.
It is the evening of Easter Sunday. The doors are closed 'out of fear of the Jews'; but behind this lies the fact that Jesus now has a form of existence to which close4 doors are irrelevant. 'Peace be with you', the fulfilment of 14 :27 ff. - 'My peace I leave to you', viz. messianic peace and salvation. Then the showing of his hands and side and the joy of the disciples (the joy of final or eschatological fulfilment; cf. 16 :16 ff.). All has been fulfilled in Christ. 'Peace' is repeated, and then comes the mandate. It is a commonplace of John that Jesus was 'sent' by the Father; so too, the disciples are sent by the risen Christ (cf. 4 :38). He has already risen, he already possesses the Spirit (cf. 7 :39) and supreme authority. As the first-fruits of a new creation he 'breathes' on them (cf. Gen. 2 :7): 'Receive the Holy Spirit; whose sins ...' This is John's giving of the Spirit, parallel to Luke's Pentecost. In Matthew and in Mark (16 :15 L), the mandate is in terms of going out to preach and baptise. In John and Luke, there is question of preaching and the forgiveness of sins. John, however, implies that the forgiveness shall be at the discretion of the apostle. Not to every-body! Does this mean that a declaration of sins, is necessary, an 'auricular confession'? Possibly. But other things have to be. considered. Matthew, for . instance, speaks of a power to bind and loose
being given to Peter (16 :19) or to the apostles or Church (18 :18). This implies a general power of bans, or authority, of admitting or not admitting, which can hardly be restricted to the forgiveness of sins. On the other hand, 1 John alludes to a continued forgiveness of sins available within . the Church (cf. 1 John 1:7-9; 2 : t2; 5 :16-17). It seems therefore that in the present passage John is thinking of a continued . ministry of forgiveness within the Church, which involves some sort of judgement or discrimination; but this cannot on the one hand be limited to the forgiveness of sins by baptism, nor on the other can it be taken to presuppose a practice declaratory confession in the Church. The precise Church situation is not clear.
The second episode belongs to the following Sunday. The point of it is not primarily the realistic demonstration that Jesus has risen from the dead, but the supreme confession of Thomas at the end, 'My Lord and my God', which is the climax of the whole
Gospel: that the Word who became flesh (1:14) has . returned to the glory that was his with the Father (17 :5) and is himself God. However, this in turn is intended as a lesson for the believing . Church: it is not those who have seen that are to be blessed; it is those who believe although they have not seen. Faith rests not on prodigies (4 :48), nor on eye-witnesses (4 :42), nor even on the Gospels, but on the word of God proclaimed in the church throughout time.
