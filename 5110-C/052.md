# The forgiven woman

In the Gospel (Luke 7 : 36-8 :3) story it is the woman who is forgiven. Traditionally she has been identified with the woman 'from whom seven demons had gone out', Mary of Magdala, and is also represented weeping over the feet of the crucified saviour; there is no basis for this tradition. More acceptable is the interpretation of her sin as being prostitution. She waits behind Jesus, and presumes to say no word to him throughout the incident, a modesty which he - with most sensitive tact - respects. At first she just stands weeping and waiting, and then suddenly is drawn by the force of her emotion to advance and kiss Ids feet.
The task of washing the feet of a guest was an essential one after a dusty journey, perhaps barefoot, but one so menial that a master was forbidden to demand that a Hebrew slave should perform it; only a despised gentile could be compelled to do it. But to omit it altogether, as Simon did, was gross incivility; the way he is immediately ready to carp at Jesus suggests also that be invited him merely to try him out or examine him rather than out of friendship.
It is one of the most frequent reproaches of the self-righteous Pharisees that Jesus does not keep himself uncontaminated from sinners, association with whom made another man unclean. The self-righteous is so contented with and wrapped-up in his own observances that he does not realize his own need for an outgoing love. Jesus teaches the lesson firstly by his parable and then in the illustration provided by the woman. Jesus' love for sinners and willingness to forgive them is perhaps especially stressed by Luke; it is he who tells the story of the prodigal son, and he alone mentions the penitent thief at the crucifixion.
