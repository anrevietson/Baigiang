# The covenant with Noah

First Reading (Gen. 9 :8-15). This is the sequel to the story of the flood. God, having accepted Noah's sacrifice as a sign and proof of man's good will, made a covenant with him. It was a unilateral agreement in which God pledged himself never again to destroy the world by flood. If the flood had signified the return of chaos to the world because of man's sinfulness, the covenant with Noah was a fresh start, a kind of new creation. As with every covenant there was an external sign as a memorial and reminder'. This time it was the rainbow. A natural phenomenon associated with the flood, it was looked on by the ancients as a bow used by God to punish man. But from now on when men saw it in the sky they were to associate it with God's mercy and recall his promise to preserve the creatures of the earth; From being a sign of impending disaster it became a reminder of God's saving presence.
This covenant with Noah was but the first in a series of alliances between God and man. Later would come the covenant with Abraham (Gen. 17) and the great covenant with Moses on Sinai (Exod. 19), which in its turn was but the forerunner of the new and everlasting covenant wrought by Christ on the cross. We who live with the benefits and blessings of Christ's covenant would do well to keep in mind the principle enshrined in all the biblical covenants. They all illustrate the way God deals with man - his justice, his mercy, and his readiness always to give him another chance. God knows and understands the weakness of man; he asks only that he does not turn away irrevocably. And that is the real significance of the new covenant made in Christ. It is God's complete and final answer to all those tentative beginnings and subsequent fresh starts made by man over the centuries. Christ is for those who need him - he is the saviour of men. A modern theologian puts it well: 'Christ is for those who, like Magdalene, know what they are capable of, and who try mightily to salvage a few sacred moments in a life of so many ambiguous years. Christ is for those who, like Paul, still tend towards those things destructive of their life and vocation' (A. Podovano, Who is Christ?, p.55). That knowledge is like the rainbow for Noah, our hope. And in it to we become more than ever aware that once 'brought to life in Christ' our Christianity must issue forth in good works here and now. Therein lies our challenge. Sadly man tends to forget it more often than not, so much so that one cannot but agree with the writer who said:
'That there was a deluge once seems not to me so great a miracle as that there is not one always.'
