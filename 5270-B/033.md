# Man and woman

First Reading (Gen 2:18-24). The Book of Genesis is an exploration of some of the most important questions of life in any age. Typically, the mode of exploration is that of narrative, rather than of the abstract reasoning which most of our western theology prefers. Israelite thought was part of the wider Mesopotamian one which produced a whole series of stories and cosmogonies, relating the experienced present to its imagined origins. Different stories, which our culture might consider contradictory, were allowed their place side by side. The totally different sequence in creation between Genesis 1, which puts the creation of man as the climax of creation, after that of the animals, and Genesis 2, which places him at the centre, and before that of the animals, is no problem for one who understands the nature of narrative and cosmogonies. Today's extract focuses on the relationship between man and woman.
Although surrounded by the wonders of God's creation - everything God made was good - the man is presented as being in incomplete while alone. He needs a companion worthy of him (a helpmate, a corruption of a help meet, i.e., a meet helper). By way of a first attempt, God fashioned wild beasts and the birds of the heavens, and brought them to man so that he would name them, thus suggesting that their identification came from man. But among even such a multitude there was no suitable helpmate. To suit man the helpmate must come from within himself. Such is the power of the attraction woman has for man, that he leaves his own father and mother to become one with her. In a manner typical of the book of Genesis, origins define reality: marriage is God's creation.
The text can be interpreted variously, from the literal to the allegorical. This is how Philo, the Alexandrian rabbi, a contemporary of Jesus, interpreted it: 'These words in their literal sense are of the nature of a myth. For how could anyone admit that a woman, or a human being at all, came into existence out of a man's side? ... And why, when there were so many parts to choose from, did he form the woman not from some other part but from the side? And which side did he take? .. What then are we to say? "Sides" is a term of ordinary life for "strength". To say that a man has "sides" is equivalent to saying that he is strong ... (Allegorical Interpretation 11.19-21). Treating some of the literature of the Bible as myth did not begin among nineteenth-century rationalists.
Responsorial Psalm (Psalm 127). The psalm reflects on the perfect bliss of those who fear the Lord and walk in his ways. Food and prosperity are guaranteed, as are the abundant fertility of wives, the peace of Jerusalem, and length of days. This psalmist's portrayal of an idyllic life for those who fear the Lord will strike many as naive.
