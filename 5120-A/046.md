# Adam and Christ

Second Reading (Rom. 5 :12-15). Paul is here concerned with showing the extent of God's love for mankind made manifest in Christ by underlining the need which man had to be saved and the transcendence of the grace of God over man's need. In order to express' the universality and radical character of man's sinful condition, Paul has recourse to the Adam typology, current in the Jewish literature of his time. It is important to appreciate the movement of Paul's thought here. Revelation of sin implies the revelation of God's saving love. We know that sin exists only because we already know that God saves us from that sin. Had Paul no realisation that it was in fact through the one man Jesus that grace, life and salvation had come into the world, he would never have thought of sin, death and condemnation entering the world through one man. It is not Adam that leads him to Christ but Christ that leads him to reflect on Adam. In other words, the burden of Paul's argument is not on Adam and his sin but upon Christ and his grace. Adam is only the background figure against which Paul draws his picture of Christ.
The Adam type enables Paul to explain how it is that all men, even independently of their own personal sins (for which, however, he does not separate the 'sin' of Adam), are under the power of sin and are thus doomed to die (not just the natural death of physical dissolution but the ultimate alienation from God). But it also serves to illustrate the supremacy of God's love and the abundance of God's grace above man's sin. Since, by definition, God's love is a saving, redemptive love, nothing can ever foil it.
