# Qualities of a Church leader

First Reading (Titus 1:1-9). (For an introduction to the Pastoral epistles 1-2 Timothy and Titus - see SCRIPTURE IN CHURCH, no.3, pp. 493 f.) Although our information about Titus, the recipient of this. letter, is scanty, we know that he was converted by St Paul (he is Paul's 'true child in a common faith', cf. 1:4), and that he later became his associate in his apostolic ministry (cf. 2 Cor. 7:6, 8:6). It is probable that Paul himself evangelized Crete and that he left Titus there as his delegate.
The introduction to the epistle (1:1-4) seems unusually solemn and formal for a letter addressed to a close friend and associate. Only at the end (v.4) does it become warm and personal. The fact that the letter was meant to be read out by Titus for his Cretan congregation may account for the elaborate construction, the formality, and the doctrinal density of the introduction. By asserting that he was dedicated to the service of God as an apostle of Jesus Christ the author indicates clearly the source of his authority. He has been given the task of ensuring that the Cretans have a genuine understanding of the apostolic message and that they live their lives in conformity with that message. The apostle is not teaching his own message but one that has been entrusted to him by God. But Paul intends to do more than warn the faithful. He wishes to promote hope among them by reminding them of the blessedness of the eternal life which Christ has promised them.
An important part of Titus's task in Crete was the organization of the Church there and the appointment of responsible ministers whose teaching and example would lead the people to a godly life. Paul gives him some advice about the presbyters or elders and about the bishops or overseers who are to have a part in the running of the Church. (The distinction between the 'presbyters' [priests] and 'bishops' in the very early Church is not clear. It is quite possible that these offices were identical or at least that they overlapped. It was only in the early second century that the two offices were clearly distinguished.) But whatever may be said about the distinction between their functions, both presbyters and bishops were representatives of God in the administration of his Church, and as such they were expected to be men of blameless character. The virtues which Titus is to look for in rulers of the community are not specifically Christian. They are rather the virtues that were currently considered desirable in any respectable and influential citizen. However, Paul stresses that since it is the task of the ministers of the Church to foster the faith of the community and to erase error they themselves must be convinced of the truth of the apostolic message and eager to spread and defend it.
