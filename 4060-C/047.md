# The Council of Jerusalem

First Reading (Acts 15 :1-2, 22-29) (H.W.). This controversy, long-buried and forgotten, and the letter which issues from it, seem at first sight startlingly irrelevant today. They represent a crucial moment in the history of the Church, for it was at this moment that it was decided whether Christianity was merely a sect within Judaism or whether it burst the bonds of Judaism altogether. It was in fact only the introduction of Gentiles into the Church which forced the Church to realise in practical terms the implications of Jesus' saying 'Nobody puts new wine into old wineskins' (Mark 2 :22). Yet those who held by the value of Jewish practices could also point to Jesus' saying 'Do not imagine that I have come to abolish the law or the prophets. I have come not to abolish but to complete them' (Mt. 5 :17). The decision reached was no mere practical matter but a profound statement of principle. The chief point of decision is that salvation is not through the law but through Christ; this is not clearly stated - perhaps it was not even formulated thus until Paul' s clarity of vision gave it such expression - but such is the implication of not saddling the Gentiles with the full burden of the law, and of the final sentence of Peter's speech at the meeting of the Church at Jerusalem, 'We are saved in the same way as they are: through the grace of the Lord Jesus' (16 :11).
The principle behind the restriction placed upon the Gentiles' liberty is also important. Probably the four 'essentials' are chosen because they are matters of ritual purity; if they were disregarded it would be impossible for Christians who were still observant Jews to associate with non-Jewish Christians because they would be ritually unclean. Certainly the first three are obviously associated with table fellowship. Thus the underlying aim of the restriction is to achieve harmony and peace in the community, by generosity on the part of the Gentiles in foregoing the freedom they would otherwise enjoy.
