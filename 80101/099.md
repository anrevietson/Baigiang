# May God let his face shine

Through the Old Testament period the priests on blessing Israel prayed that God would let his face shine on them and bring them peace (first reading; Responsorial psalm); in 'the fullness of time' this prayer was fulfilled, through the birth of Jesus from Mary (second reading). . The shepherds, who first saw the fulfilment of the promises, found Jesus with Mary his mother (Gospel).
First Reading (Num. 6 :22-27). In this first reading we have the formula used by the Jewish priests ('Aaron and his sons') when they called down God's blessing on Israel. Their prayer was that God would bless Israel and keep it, that is that he would bring it happiness and well-being, and protect it through his divine providence. They prayed that God would let his face shine on his people, by being gracious to them. God was the source of life and light. To pray that his face shine on men was to ask God to let them experience his goodness, his loving providence; to pray God to bring peace to men, peace in the rich Jewish meaning of that word, i.e. happiness, well-being - both material and spiritual; in short the good life that God had intended for men from the beginning.
One may legitimately ask why this reading was chosen for today's celebration. What has the blessing 'formula of Israel's priesthood to do with the Solemnity of Mary, the Mother of God. There is, of course, no direct connection. Yet, as indicated above in the introductory note, the first reading is well chosen if we keep in mind the guiding theme of today's liturgical readings, which is the fulfilment of the promises through Jesus, born of Mary. In other words, we must approach the first reading not in itself as part of the book of Numbers, nor as a formula given to Israel by Moses. Rather should we consider the teaching it enshrines and its relation to the promises and the covenant. The priestly blessing is really timeless in its sentiments and fits badly into its present context in Numbers, chapter six, after a long, legalistic passage on the Nazirite vow. (Its original position may have been after Lev. 9 :22, where Aaron's blessing is first mentioned.) What the priests invoked in their blessing was really what revealed religion was all about: divine favour and friendship and the happiness and well-being flowing from these. The blessing, of its very nature, harked back to the promises made to Abraham and to the covenant made on Sinai. It was a prayer that God's fidelity to these would be made known.
The blessing was part of the Temple and Synagogue liturgies. It was a sacred text, with the result that when in later times the Bible was translated from Hebrew into the Aramaic vernacular this blessing continued to be pronounced in Hebrew, without translation. Down through her history, as Israel awaited the fulfilment of the promises, she continued to recite this prayer. The prayer itself makes no mention of the fulfilment or of the Messiah. But both can be read in it. Fulfilment of the prayer would only come with the advent of messianic times.
Today's Responsorial Psalm (Ps. 66) takes up the sentiments of the priestly blessing and spells them out at greater length. Through this psalm the liturgy further emphasised the message of the priestly blessing. But the psalm d6es more. Whereas the first reading restricts the blessing to Israel ('This is how you are to bless the sons of Israel'), the psalm has a universal outlook. It prays that all nations may experience God's saving help, that the ends of the earth may revere him. In other words it looks forward to the messianic salvation and asks God to usher it in.
Second Reading (Gal. 4 :4-7). Paul takes up where the Responsorial psalm leaves off. With Christ and in Christ the appointed time has come. God, who spoke to Israel at different times and in different ways, has spoken finally and definitively in his own son Jesus. In the same epistle to the Galatians Paul explains what all this means for mankind. In Christ the promises made to Abraham have been fulfilled, promises which were intended for all peoples, not for Israel alone. And fulfilment means that we are sons of God, brothers of Christ and co-heirs with him. Believers are made conscious of the reality of this new liberty and dignity by the Holy Spirit who leads them to pray in the very words Jesus addressed to his Father: 'Abba, Father.' It is a bold thing to use such an intimate term as 'Father' when addressing God. We need the example of Jesus and the inspiration of the Holy Spirit to do it. Such is the dignity of the new state of man that has come with Jesus. And in sending his Son among us to so renew humanity, God willed that he be born of a woman, of Mary. This is Paul's sole reference to Our Lady. Yet it is one which links Mary directly with the order of salvation. This is Mary's dignity: she was the mother of him who was sent to set us free.
