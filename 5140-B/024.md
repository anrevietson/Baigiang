# The call of Ezekiel

First Reading (Ezek. 2:2-5). These verses are taken from the account of Ezekiel's call to the prophetic mission. It was a time of crisis in the history of the chosen people. The great religious reform begun by King Josiah about the year 621 BC had lapsed after the death of the king in 609. From then on, the path went steeply downhill for Judah and Jerusalem. Idolatrous corruptions crept into the religious practices once again. On the political front, an unwise attempt to escape from vassalage to the Babylonian Empire finally brought a Babylonian army under the walls of Jerusalem in 598. The young King Jehoiachin had no choice but to surrender the city. The royal family and officials together with large numbers of the nobility and skilled craftsmen were exiled to Babylon. Among the exiles was a young man from a Jerusalem priestly family, named Ezekiel. Five years later (Ezek. 1:2) Ezekiel received the call to undertake the heavy burden of a prophet's work. The first chapter of the book tells of a strange and colourful vision which impresses on Ezekiel the holy transcendence of God and his own nothingness in comparison. He falls awe-struck to the ground. The Lord then speaks to Ezekiel (our passage), addressing him as 'son of man' -a designation which stresses the lowliness of man made from the dust of the earth. So crushed is Ezekiel by the tremendous vision that he finds it beyond his own power to raise himself up; God infuses special strength ('the Spirit') into his weak body, and Ezekiel stands to hear the word of God.
It is a hard word, for the hearts of the chosen people are far from God. But it is at the same time a word of love, for it means that God will not abandon his people. He will send yet again a spokesman of his to bring his word before the people in their desperate situation. Whether they listen or refuse to listen, the prophet will be sent. Ezekiel's was indeed a hard task, one which strained human nature to its limits. He had to become totally indifferent to public opinion, 'hard as a diamond' (Ezek. 3:9), as he uncompromisingly delivered God's word. All that mattered was that the people should know without possibility of evasion that 'there was a prophet among them', that the holy God was still concerned about them.
Ezekiel's life and mission is a constant lesson for us that the proclaiming of God's word is a serious and demanding task. God's spokesmen must be prepared for opposition, misunderstanding and rejection; it was so for the Old Testament prophets, it was so for Jesus himself (see today's Gospel), and it is so for the Church. But the call and the sending come from God and he gives his Spirit to strengthen and enlighten those who accept this task.
Responsorial Psalm (Ps. 122). This is the prayer of men who suffer from the arrogant scorn of proud and rich oppressors. The mood of the prayer is one of quiet trust that God is concerned for the oppressed. Filled with this hope they look to God. So too does the Church when faced by human opposition to the message of God's love in Christ.
