# The crucifixion

The Gospel (Luke 23 :35-43). It is normal nowadays to recognize that each evangelist has his own particular picture of Jesus, which we can arrive at by comparing the accounts of the same incident in the different Gospels. Luke goes out of his way to exonerate the people from blame ('and the people stood by, watching, but the rulers scoffed ...', v. 35), and indeed they return to Jerusalem 'beating their breasts' (v.48). It is the rulers and the soldiers who are the culprits, although the centurion praised God after what he had seen (v.47). Moreover, Luke presents Jesus on the cross as exercising, or rather continuing to exercise, forgiveness. He asks the Father to forgive them, for they do not know what they do (cf. Stephen's prayer, Acts 7 :60). Luke alone has this prayer for forgiveness, and its inclusion is very much in line with his portrait of Jesus as the great forgiver of those who trespass against him (Luke alone has the parable of the Good Samaritan, 10 :29-37, Lost Sheep, Lost Coin, Prodigal Father, 15, Pharisee and Publican, 18 :9-14). . Rather than attribute their actions to malice, Jesus excuses them on the grounds of ignorance - a factor which was given due weight in the early preaching of the Church (cf. Acts 3 :17; 13 :27). Jesus bore the inscription 'This is the King of the Jews' on the cross. It was both his honour and his shame: to have claimed kingship was his greatest crime (23 :2-4), and it was now an object of ridicule for the ignorant soldiers. However, the 'good thief' recognized Jesus (the Saviour) behind the shame of the cross, and asked him for a remembrance in his kingdom. Jesus assured him that he would share in his glory, just as he had shared in his suffering. For Luke, therefore, Christ is king on the cross, from which he mediates salvation for others.
