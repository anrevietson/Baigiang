# The fast that pleases God

First reading (Is. 58:1-14) (Friday and Saturday). The final section of the book of Isaiah (Is. 55-66), or Third Isaiah as it is called, dates from about 500 BC. It was addressed to Israelites who had returned from the Babylonian exile and settled down in Palestine. They had returned with the hope that political power would be theirs in a prosperous Jewish community. But they were soon disillusioned. They experienced poverty and hardship, and the expected universal reign of Yahweh showed no sign of becoming a reality. Many wondered why the promises made by the prophets were being delayed and why their own prayers and sufferings were in vain. Isaiah 58:3 reflects this mentality : ,Why have we fasted and thou seest not? Why have we humbled ourselves and thou takest no knowledge of it.'
Some of the better-off Israelites took advantage of the misery of their poorer brothers and tried to profit from it. The greater part of our present chapter attacks these oppressors who try to hide their greed and inhumanity behind a facade of fasting and piety. The prophet points out that zeal for pious practices that is not coupled with a genuine solicitude for the well-being of man is not true devotion. God favours those who try to lighten the burden of their fellow-men by attending to their daily needs. Our Lord was later to promise the kingdom to these same people (Mt. 24:34).
In verses nine to twelve the prophet tries to raise the morale of the people and to inspire in them hope for the future. The new upsurge of vitality and fruitfulness which God will give is pictured in the traditional imagery of the Old Testament. The concept of a watered garden suggests prosperity (cf. Ps. 1:3). The rebuilding of ancient ruins implies power and political freedom. These images together with the general idea of God guiding his community and satisfying their desire with good things were calculated to give new hope to a despondent people.
In verse thirteen we notice the influence of the spirit of legalism that marked the post-exilic period and prepared for the casuistry that was condemned by our Lord. But this verse with its stress on sabbath observance should not distract us from the main teaching of the chapter which condemns the emptiness of external observance that is not inspired by a true understanding of God and of his demands. The whole chapter breathes an air of kindliness and brotherhood that reminds one of the gospel spirit.
