# God so loved the world

The Gospel (John 3 :14-21). This is the final section of the 'Nicodemus discourse', in which Jesus explains how man can (and ought) be born anew. It is difficult to estimate how much of this section is a report of what our Lord actually said at some occasion and how much is St John's added theological reflection. At any rate, the discourse states fundamental Christian beliefs about Jesus the lifesaver, about salvation through faith in him, and about the judgement that condemns those who knowingly reject his gospel. Nicodemus is a well-disposed) quintessential Jew. As a Pharisee, he professe4 scrupulous devotion to the Mosaic law; being a Rabbi, he was recognised as a religious teacher; and the title 'ruler of the Jews' (John 3 :1) indicates that he was probably a member of the Sanhedrin, the highest Jewish governing body. Therefore the dialogue has the symbolic force of an appeal by Christ to the Jewish people, to understand the true Spirit and plan of God. Like each individual the Jewish nation must undergo a rebirth, in order to enter the kingdom of God.
The dialogue changes to a monologue, at verse twelve. From here on, Jesus (or St John) speaks in a way which it would be difficult for Nicodemus to follow. He tells of the 'Son of Man', who has descended from heaven to reveal heavenly things. Then comes the text of today's Gospel, which centres upon the 'lifting-up' or exaltation of that Son of Man.
John gives double significance to the verb hypsonsthenai ('to be lifted up'). First, it is the physical fact of being elevated upon the cross, just as the bronze statue of a serpent was raised by Moses over the bitten Israelites (Num. 21 :4-9); but it also involves the spiritual elevation of Jesus by the Father - that glorification which was granted him through his death. Like the bronze statue of old, the raised-up Son of Man will be an effective sign of salvation, for all who look on him with trust. (N.B. Wisd. 16 :6 actually calls the bronze serpent a 'symbol of salvation'.)
Verses 16-21 sound like a section from a sermon preached by St John, rather than something spoken by Jesus. Yet it develops further upon what has gone before. The hope of salvation offered through the descent and exaltation of the Son of Man, is now traced back to its ultimate source, viz. God's almighty love. It was God who set the process in motion, wishing that the world be saved from sin. Verse 16 ('God 56 loved the world....') is the very foundation of the Gospel; together with John 1:14 ('The Word was made flesh') and John 12 :32 ('when I am lifted up, I will draw all men to myself'), it constitutes the 'good news' proclaimed by John (and other apostles as well).
Yet, not everyone will benefit by this loving initiative of God; some, by hardening their hearts against belief in Jesus, will seal their own condemnation. For Jesus brings judgement with him -necessarily. He is like a light that shines into the heart of each man, and the way we react to this light determines our destiny. If, loving darkness and deceit and selfish ways, we turn away wilfully from God's light, we cannot come to God; we renounce the right to be called his children. For St John, salvation or damnation begins for a man already, here on earth, according to his acceptance or rejection of Jesus, the only Son of God. This is his 'scatology', which makes a smooth link between life here and now, and life in eternity. The essential judgement is not passed by Christ, but is contained in the choice we make when we're confronted with him.
It may be difficult to unify all the readings today in one homily; if one wished to do so, the evident link is 'grace' - the generosity of God. As he saved the exiles from their distress, despite their past sins, so he wishes to bring us out of the darkness of sin into the light of his own life, through faith in Jesus Christ whom he has sent.
