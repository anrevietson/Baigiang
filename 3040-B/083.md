# Exile and return

First Reading (2 Chron. 36 :14-16, 19-23). Until recently the books of Chronicles (Hebrew title: The Events of the Days) were known in Catholic circles as 'Paralipomena' or Leftovers, from their rather uncomplimentary Greek title. The reason for this is that these two books were thought to fill out many details and genealogies omitted from the books of Samuel and Kings, which cover somewhat the same period. In fact, our author ('the Chronicler') had a slightly different theological outlook from that of the other historian ('the Deuteronomist'). Instead of placing his major emphasis upon the Sinai covenant, he passes over this event in silence, and devotes his attention to God's promise to David The reigns of David and Solomon are reported at some length, and after that the focus remains upon the kings of Judah, descended from David, through whom God's favour or disfavour would come upon his people most exegetes would connect Ezra with authorship of the Chronides and date the composition around 400 BC.
Although one main purpose of Chronicles is to glorify the house of David, and the Jerusalem temple built by David's son, yet he faithfully reports the wickedness of various Davidic kings Rehoboam (2 Chron. 12) and his punishment (13 :20) is a case in point:
despite his great military . strength, he displeased Yahweh by forsaking the law, and was struck down by divine justice. Likewise Asa (2 Chron. 16) and Uzziah (2 Chron. 26) were humbled for their sins. This insistence on the punitive justice of God prepares the way for interpreting the disastrous Babylonian exile as God's just chastisement for the continued obstinate infidelity of both the kings and populace of Judah (2 Chron. 36 :15-21). The Chaldeans are the sword of God, carrying out .his sentence against the faithless city and sanctuary.
The Chronicler, however, ends on an optimistic key. Jerusalem is not destined for oblivion; God's promises are not nullified by his . people's sins; his mercy overcomes his wrath. Thus h& brings the exile to an end, by means of another instrument, king Cyrus of Persia. Today's reading shows us this foreign monarch in the act of ordering the rebirth of Jerusalem and its worship. Despite setbacks, the life of God's people will continue; significantly, it is with this passage that the Hebrew canon . of the Old Testament ends, pointing forward in hope towards an unforeseen future.
Responsorial Psalm (Ps. 136). The fierce loyalty of the exiled Jews to their homeland, finds expression in this psalm. They waited in a mixture of grief and hope for the day of their release from Babylon. For us, this devotion to the Holy Land has a message too: we are not fully at home, until we reach the presence of Christ in heaven. During this life we are like 'strangers and exiles', waiting for the Lord's call to come home at last: let us prize the kingdom of heaven above all other joys!
