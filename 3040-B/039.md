# Exile and return

First Reading (2 Chron 36:14-16, 19-23). The second book of Kings ends rather abruptly with the account of the release of the exiled king Jehoiachin from prison at the beginning of the reign of the Babylonian king Evil Merodach, successor to Nebuchadnezzar. This was in 562 BC. This tit bit of information was itself a glimmer of hope that all was not lost, but it indicates that the historical narrative of Samuel-Kings which it brings to a close seems to have been completed between 562 and 539, the date of the fall of Babylon to Cyrus the Persian and of Cyrus's decree permitting the Jews to return to their homeland. The books of Chronicles, covering the same period as Samuel and Kings, is a much later composition, and 2 Chronicles is the last book of the Hebrew Bible. After the sad event of the exile, the narrative could not very well be allowed to end in a note of apparent despair for God's covenanted people. Thus it is that at the end of the present book of Chronicles (found also in Ezra 1:1-4) we are given Cyrus' decree permitting the exiled Jews return to their homeland and rebuild their temple. The inevitability of exile had been foretold by the prophets. But so too was God's plan that this exile was to be of limited duration, after which sacred history would run the course set for it by God. The prophet Jeremiah had so indicated; see Jer 25:11-12; 29:10. God's word, spoken through his prophets, would be fulfilled.
Second Reading Eph 2:4-10). A message of the first reading is that the very survival of God's covenanted people was due solely to a free act of divine grace. The theme of this second reading is that this holds for the entire plan of salvation through Christ. This is due to God's grace alone, not to any good work on our part.
