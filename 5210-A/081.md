# You are Peter

The Gospel (Mat. 16:13-20). These verses of Matthew's Gospel have taken on such apologetic Seriousness, because of the promise to Peter, that their context in the Gospel has been forgotten. To use them as a jumping board for a Sermon on the Holy Father would not do justice to the text nor to the office the Holy Father holds.
Since the parables sermon, St Matthew has been concerned with the delineation of the Kingdom of God. It is a mysterious, divine reality; it takes Origin in Israel, humble beginnings indeed compared with its future glory. Above all, however, Matthew does not let us forget that the Kingdom has concrete expression within the world; men are nourished within it, they embrace it in the hope of healing and divine protection. Again we might ask: can we not be more precise? What is the Kingdom? Today's reading forms a climax; Matthew has thus far hinted, suggested, led on. The question about the kingdom is rephrased: Who do people say the Son of Man is?' The evangelist tells us it is not What is the Kingdom', but Who is the Kingdom' that is important. When we ask the question about Jesus aright, then we can get the Kingdom right. Jesus embodies this Kingdom; everything about it refers to himself. (Thus St John can rephrase his parables: I am the living bread, the light of the world, the sheep gate, etc.)
All power in this Kingdom is invested in Christ. All its movements, energy, position flows from him. Once the faith of Peter has opened the apostle's heart to Jesus and so brought him into the Kingdom, the Kingdom's power can flow into him, and, from Christ's own lips, it flows into him in a special Way. Christ's promise to Peter is not about privilege to possess as much as duty to perform. As before in this Gospel, the importance of faith is paramount, as is the conviction that this faith comes from God alone. Perhaps Matthew argues consistently with his Jewish upbringing. Entry to Israel was by divine gift in one's very birth; now the new birth into the new Israel is equally gift, the gift of God's revelation accepted, which we call faith. Even in this stylised passage, one has the distinct impression that Peter was a very good believer. His position in the Church depended entirely on his faith being actually the bed-rock of his companions. Was his promised office then conditional? Hardly, if we seriously accept that faith is a gift of God.
