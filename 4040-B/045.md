# The good shepherd

Gospel (Jn 10:11-18). The discourse on the Good Shepherd (10:1-21) follows the sign of healing of the man born blind in chapter 9. Both take place between the Jewish feasts of Tabernacles and of Dedication, celebrated in the autumn and early winter respectively. John seems to structure his gospel to develop the symbolism of the feasts and to continue the contrast between the authorities in the previous episode and the shepherd in this chapter. While the authorities are blind guides who stumble and fail in their responsibilities, the shepherd is a good, knowing and faithful leader. Within this discourse, descriptions provide vivid contrast: thieves and bandits/gatekeeper; voice of strangers/voice of shepherd; hired hand who does not care/shepherd who will lay down his life. Darkness and blindness are a compelling backdrop for the presentation of Jesus as light and life in this section of the gospel.
The metaphor of sheep, gate and pasture, as well as the challenge of authorities, is a clear reminder of Ezekiel 34. While the Jewish tradition utilizes the good shepherd theme in a variety of contexts (Gen 49:24; Ps 23) and describes its leaders as shepherds (2 Sam 7:7-9; Num 27:16-17), the laying down of the shepherd's life is a new dimension in the Christian Scriptures (Mk 6:34; Lk 15:3-7; Mt 25:32-34). This sacrifice, explicitly mentioned in verses 11,15,17, and 18, is a free choice of Jesus and an act of love. The sacrifice of the shepherd results in unity, one flock and one leader.
John also uses the expression 'I AM' in this discourse (10:11,14). Within the gospel, this expression, spoken by Jesus, is explicitly used as the name of God (Jn 8:24,28, 58; 13:19). However, in this selection, the statement implies divine commitment since the Father sends the Son. Jesus witnesses to the unity of Father and Son within the gospel, and so pro-claims: 'I AM' the bread of life (6:35,51), the light of the world (8:12; 9:5), the gate for the sheep (10:7,9), the good shepherd (10:11,14), the way, truth and life (14:6), and the true vine (15:1,5). This revelation of Jesus' true identity and mission leads to a crisis of faith for those who hear the word. The decision results either in discipleship within the community of faith or remaining outside the sheepfold. While John clearly contrasts the voice of the shepherd and that of the stranger, the decision to follow the true shepherd requires a discerning and loving faith. Entrance into the community is through Jesus, and this connection between our vision of Church and our understanding of Christ is a basic element in the gospel. Christians are a community of believers in Jesus.
