# The good shepherd

The Gospel (Jn. 10 :11-18). This passage explains the little parable contained in 10 3b-5 concerning the relation between the shepherd and the sheep just as vv. 1-10 were an explanation of the parable in 10 : 1-3a concerning the way to approach the sheep. Jesus has called himself the gate of the sheepfold. New he calls himself the good shepherd, the true shepherd who can enter openly through the . gate of the sheepfold. The good shepherd is one who (a) is willing to die to protect his sheep, (b) knows his sheep intimately.
The imagery of shepherding appealed to the Israelite mind because of its historical pastoral background. Yahweh himself was regarded as the shepherd of the people (cf. Gen. 49 :24) and the kings, representing Yahweh, also came to be referred to as shepherds of the people. Today's Gospel particularly brings to mind a passage from Ezek. 34 where God promised his people, first of all, that he himself would become their shepherd and then that he would choose a shepherd for them in the messianic age. Christ's assertion that he is the good shepherd indicates that this age has arrived, that he is the promised one.
In Palestine, where the sheep were at times in real danger from wild animals, the possibility of the good shepherd having to lay down his life in their defence would not be so remote as it might appear to us, but the real meaning of the passage is seen when it is applied to Jesus himself who did in fact lay down his life for the salvation of his followers. This is in marked contrast to the Pharisees, the hirelings, who cared little or nothing for the salvation of their people.
The second characteristic of the true shepherd is that he knows his sheep intimately and they know him. To know in the biblical sense Is the result of personal contact and experience arid develops into love. The mutual knowledge of Jesus and those belonging to him is an extension of the mutual knowledge of Father and Son (cf. Jn. 17 :25-26). A more intimate knowledge of God was to be a characteristic of the new covenant foretold by Jeremiah (cf. Jer. 31:34) and the covenant idea is present also in the passage from Ezekiel mentioned above.
The Father delights in the obedience of the Son who becomes obedient 'even to accepting death', but death is not the end. In John's view, Christ is glorified in his death. It was when raised on the cross he would draw all men to himself (Jn. 12 :32); it was at the moment of death he poured out the Spirit (cf. Jn. 19 :30 and 7 :39). Finally the passage emphasises the freedom of Jesus in going to his death.
