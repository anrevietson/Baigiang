# The resurrection of the dead

First Reading (Dan. 12 :1-3). It is generally agreed that the book of Daniel was written about 165 BC shortly before the death of the Greek emperor (Seleucid dynasty), Antiochus Epiphanes The collapse of Jerusalem in 587 ushered in a long period of history during which the Jews were the pawns of various international superpowers: the exile in Babylon was ended after the Persian king Cyrus the Great, captured Babylon in 539, but in 331 Persian domination yielded to that of the Greeks under Alexander the Great. Despite their being under foreign rule during all of this period, the Jews in Palestine enjoyed some political, and effectively full religious freedom. This lasted until the rise to power of Antlochus Epiphanes in 175 BC. He tried to force the Jews to abandon their traditional faith and to practise the common faith of his empire. This conflict between fidelity to their religious traditions and submission to the demands of the foreign pagan ruler is the political and religious background against which the book of Daniel was written. The author sets out to show that it is the God of Israel who controls history ('he removes kings and sets up kings', 2 :21), and guides the world of changing political fortunes until the final consummation of history at the end-time. This message had a particular relevance for a people under a two-pronged attack from the allurements of a rich pagan culture, and the persecution by their ruler, and its use in the liturgy emphasises its importance for us today. In chapter twelve the author moves on to describe the divinely-appointed end-time. The Opening verses complete the vision of the author described in chapters ten and following, and bring these chapters to a very optimistic conclusion. Israel will be at the centre of the new order through its representative, Michael, the patron of Israel. Although disaster will be apparent on all sides, the people of Israel, or, at least, those whose names are inscribed in the Book of the Living (Ps. 69 :28; Ex. 32 :32 f.), will be saved. Those who had stood firm, even to the point of martyrdom, in the struggle against the persecution of Antiochus Epiphanes would be 'awakened from their sleep' (cf. John 11 :11 ff.) to live an eternal life (the first occurrence of the term in the Bible), while those who capitulated would rise to shame and everlasting contempt. This is the first clear statement of the resurrection of the dead in the Bible:
it was the confidence of the author in Yahweh's justice that spurred the author on to the point of making such a bold prediction. The wise leaders and teachers of the people would shine like stars in the new order of things. It would not be correct to read later dogmatic developments about heaven and hell into this statement, but it is clear that the author prepared the way for the development of the doctrines of judgement, eternal life for the righteous, and eternal damnation for the wicked - an end-time that would not bring reward for the uprightness of the virtuous and condemnation for the wicked, would, in the author's view be less than worthy of the justice of God.
The Responsorial Psalm (Ps. 16 :5, 8-11). Although the text is vague as to precisely what sort of liberation from death is meant (from sudden death, or from corruption, or from the nether world), the New Testament applies vv. 8-11 to Jesus' triumph over death (Acts 2 :25-28), and the Christian today can pray this psalm from a much richer theological background than that available to the psalmist.
