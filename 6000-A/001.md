# Arise, Jerusalem

First Reading (Isaiah 60 :1-6). This is part of a long poem which is addressed to Jerusalem and describes its restoration after the Exile. It comes from the later chapters commonly attributed to a Second or possibly a Third Isaiah, the work of a later disciple; at any rate, in the spirit of the original prophet. The general theme of these chapters is consolation and hope.
The opening verses quoted here sound the key-note of joy and give it a universal bearing with the recurring reference to peoples and nations. Jerusalem restored will be the centre of a new and greater Israel - the Church.
The vocabulary is full of words such as 'Light', 'Radiance', 'Glory', which suggest the new revelation which God will make to his people, and whose climax will be the Incarnation. The contrast between 'light' and 'darkness' points forward to St John's Prologue, as well as back to Isaiah 9: 'The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light.'
In sum, the message is: the light of deliverance has dawned for Israel, and all nations will benefit by its radiance (a figure of the redemption). The exiles are pictured as gathering for the return to Jerusalem, bringing with them the wealth of the nations (a figure of the reversal of the Babylonian captivity). The Eastern nations bring appropriate gifts of gold and frankincense (suggesting the gifts of the Magi) - the following verses describe the western nations in similar vein.
Though addressed to Jerusalem, or to Israel, as God's Chosen People, now restored to their traditional place, the prophecy will be fulfilled in Christ ('the Light of the World') and in the new Israel, the Church. In its universalist or world-view of salvation, it is appropriately used here as a link-up with the story of Christ's recognition by the Magi as representing the 'Nations.'
The Responsorial Psalm (71) is a royal psalm (applicable to Christ), whose theme of the universality of salvation to the poor and the needy (the Anaheim of Old Testament piety) echoes the words of Isaiah (above).
