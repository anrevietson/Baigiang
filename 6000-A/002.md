# The Magi

The Gospel (Matthew 2 :1-12). This is the well known story of the Magi or the wise men (Astrologers? Essences?) from the East (Arabia? Mesopotamia?) who came to worship the newly born Christ-child in Bethlehem, bringing the kind of (symbolic) gifts already foreshadowed in Isaiah (first reading above).
A good deal is being said and written about the story of the Magi which would classify it as a Jewish literary form known as Midrash, and would therefore regard it as fiction. This is really a technical question and should not affect the message of the passage, which is the preacher's real concern. However, it should be pointed out, first, that the term Midrash needs careful definition, and that the story of the Magi does not really correspond to its strict sense, which means the elaboration of a biblical text (Old Testament in fact) in order to draw out further meanings of it or to apply it to new circumstances. This is not what Matthew is doing; he is rather describing a new event in sacred history. The story he tells fits into the known facts about Herod's character and reign in Palestine at the time, and there is no convincing reason for dismissing it completely as unhistorical. While the literary form of the story does suggest a parallel with the narrative kind of Midrash which the Jews used to elaborate their Scriptures, this does not mean that the narrative itself (or even any of its details, such as the star) is purely fictional. If anything, what Matthew is employing here is the particular type of Midrash known as Pesher: the telling of a story in such a way as to show how it fulfilled the Scriptures. In fact, he quotes a fulfilment text - vs. 5-6 - one of four in the Infancy narrative - in order to point to the Messianic character of the Child with a prophetic text from Micah 5 1 (a contemporary of Isaiah). Whatever about the technical character (midrashic or not) of this episode, its main themes are the royal Messiahship of Jesus and its recognition by the Gentiles. Note how all the details (the strange visitors from the East, the mysterious star, the dream, the gifts, all lead up to the final gesture of homage and worship (the Greek word here is proskynesia=adoration). The adoration of the Child Jesus by the Magi fulfils Isaiah's prophecy (first reading) of the homage to be paid by the nations to the true Israel in the person of the Messiah.
The gifts of the Magi correspond to those listed by Isaiah (above), bringing out the universalist significance of this event (the tribute of the nations). The gifts themselves may be symbolic:
it has been suggested, for instance, that the gold signifies royalty, the frankincense priesthood, and the myrrh suffering - all of them characteristic of the Messiah.
Note, finally, how in this story of the Magi Matthew skilfully presents the idea of a universal Saviour who is ironically recognised by the Gentiles rather than by the Jews - a theme which he goes on to develop in the Gospel as a whole. On our recognition of Christ as God and Saviour, our duty to worship him appropriately, our bringing of 'gifts' at Mass, might be fruitfully developed out of today's liturgical readings.
