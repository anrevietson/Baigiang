# The wedding feast

The Gospel (Mat. 22:1-14). Jesus continues his controversy with the Jews in the parable of the wedding feast. The similarity between this parable and the parable of the vineyard which it follows in Matthew are striking (see last Sunday's Gospel): in both, two groups of servants are sent; those to whom the servants are sent refuse to do what the servants say, and kill some of them; those who refuse to obey are destroyed and they are replaced by others who make up for what the first lot have failed to do.
Clearly Matthew means the two parables to be understood in the same way: he is describing God's manner of dealing with the Jews, their disobedience, and the new covenant which will include the Gentiles.
The first ten verses of today's reading have a parallel in Luke 14:16-24. The two versions of the story have a common basis:
the invitation to the feast, the refusal of those who were invited, the invitation then issued to all and sundry and the room filled with guests.
Matthew strongly accentuates the messianic character of the parable. The one who issues the invitation is a king, and the occasion is a marriage feast. Matthew has two groups of servants to summon the guests: the first, the Old Testament prophets; the second, the apostolic missionaries. Those who are invited are not content to decline the invitation: they murder the messengers. When the king's patience is finally exhausted he takes a terrible revenge: death for the murderers and ruin for their city. The subsequent destruction of Jerusalem by the forces of Vespasian in AD 70 would give the words a significance perhaps missed by their first hearers.
Matthews final scene (vv. 11-14) is really a separate parable added to the first. The king Who has invited the guests comes into the hall to inspect them; he finds one who has no wedding garment and commands him to be thrown out. (This must originally have been a separate parable; how could a guest be blamed for not being correctly dressed for a Wedding, when he has been brought along in the way described in the previous parable?)
Conditions have changed. Now there is question of the morality of individuals within the newly-chosen group. The messianic kingdom on earth becomes the perfect kingdom of heaven. The banqueting hall is the life of the age to come; outside it men are lost. Anyone without a 'wedding garment' is spiritually unprepared and unworthy to enter. Mere membership of the Church is not enough. Ours must be a living faith resulting in Works of love.
