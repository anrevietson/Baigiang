# Manna in the wilderness

First Reading (Exodus 16:2-3, 12-15). Set in the period between the escape from Egypt and the covenant cutting at Mount Sinai (Ex. 15:22-18:27). The general impression is of a rag-taggle bunch of grumpy wanderers, forever complaining about their hard jot. One such complaint - basically a hankering after the fleshpots of Egypt - evokes this response: the quail and the manna (see also Numbers 11:4-9). Both phenomena have received scientific explanation. Quail, in the course of their long migrations between Africa and Europe, are frequently forced down to rest in the Sinai peninsula, and so are easily caught. The manna is most probably a secretion produced by certain scale insects from the Tararish tree (tamarix mannifera); this secretion, which has a sweet taste, falls to the ground and hardens during the night and may be gathered before it melts in the heat of the morning sun. Whatever its factual basis, however, this has become for the Hebrews the classic instance of God's care for his people (cf. Ps. 78:17-28 - 'the wheat of heaven ... the bread of the mighty'; Ps. 105:40 - 'the bread of heaven'; Wis. 16 :20 - 'the food of angels ... omne delectamentum Zn se habentem'; Neh. 9:15 - 'bread from heaven'). In Exodus 16:32-34, Moses orders that some of this manna be preserved as a memorial of the event just as the tablets of the law are to be preserved in the Ark of the Covenant (cf. Ex. 25 :16). They are both symbols of the active love of God. So this feeding with quail and manna is much more than a mere satisfying of hunger. It is one episode in the great event of covenant-making whereby these nondescript refugees from Egypt become the chosen people of Yahweh (see Ex. 19-20; Deut. 5-7). The feeding with manna is, in a sense, part of Israel's birthright (see today's responsorial psalm from Ps. 77); the covenant relationship set up between Yahweh and his people, and his concern for them, is to endure from generation to generation.
