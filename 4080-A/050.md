# The coming of the Spirit at Pentecost

First Reading (Acts 2 :1-11). The story of the coming of the Spirit at Pentecost is no naive description, but is packed with theological significance. The basic sense is to be found in the fact that what happened is seen as repeating the events at the giving of the law on Mount Sinai, which marked in a way the birth of the people of God just as this incident marks the birth of the Church; but at the same time the event has a specifically Christian character. According to Jewish tradition at the giving of the law there came a mighty sound which turned to fire from which issued a voice proclaiming the law. The fire therefore acts as the vehicle for what was constitutive of the people of God, the law, as now it is the vehicle of the Spirit which is the life-principle of the Church. In another version the fire split into seventy tongues of fire, corresponding to the seventy nations of the world (according to Jewish reckoning); thus there is built into the Jewish tradition also the missionary dimension which is given in Luke' s version by the presence of the different peoples. For they too represent all nations of the world; the list seems to be founded on a list of countries which corresponds to the signs of the zodiac and stand for all lands under heaven.
What gives the incident its specifically Christian colouring is the fire and the speaking in tongues. Fire was to be a sign of the last times even in the Old Testament, where it was to refine and test God's people at the coming of the Messiah; this was picked up by John the Baptist. Jesus says that he has 'come to bring fire on the earth' and desires that it may be kindled (Luke 12 :49). This is perhaps the occasion on which it is kindled, the baptism 'with the Holy Spirit and with fire' of which the Baptist spoke (Mat; 3 :11). Speaking in tongues is a sign of the presence of the Spirit and of the Spirit's transcendence of human limitations which occurs frequently in the early Church. Usually in the New Testament this speaking in tongues is unintelligible speech which needs to be interpreted by one who has a corresponding gift of the Spirit (1 Cor. 14:27-28), and it seems to be a spontaneous outpouring whose function is solely praise of God by the speaker. But here it is intelligible to those whose home languages are highly varied, so that Luke seems to combine this spontaneous praise of God in the Spirit with the first missionary effort of the nascent Church.
Luke's message in this passage is, then, that on this day the last times begin, the time of the Church which today receives the Spirit, its life-principle. There are present witnesses from the whole world to which it is destined to spread, and they see the ebullient power of God at work in the Church.
