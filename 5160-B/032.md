# Of sheep and shepherds

First Reading (Jer. 23:1-6). 'Of Sheep and Shepherds' - a classical biblical image used to devastating effect by Jeremiah in today's first reading. At the end of a series of condemnations of Judah's unfaithful kings (chapter twenty-two), the prophet pronounces His savage accusation - those whose duty it was to care did not care; the shepherds have failed. The context is that of the Babylonian captivity (587-538 BC), and typically the reasons for this disaster are seen as being not military or economic but religious. The failure of the people to remain faithful to the Sinai covenant is focused in the failure of their kings - they have allowed 'the flock of my pasture to be destroyed and scattered' (23:1; see Ezekiel 34 for a similar condemnation). But it is not the threat of punishment that forms the heart of Jeremiah's proclamation, but the promise of rescue. The shepherds have failed and the sheep are scattered; well, Yahweh himself will be their shepherd. And the theme of Yahweh the Shepherd appears (v.3), a theme which surfaces at many points throughout the prophets (cf. Is. 40:11, 56:8; Zech. 10:8 etc.) and the psalms (notably in Psalm 22 - today's Responsorial Psalm). It is this rampant hope that forms the kernel of today's reading, and it leads on to an oracle (vv. 5-6) in which the leadership of the people comes not from 'new shepherds' (v.4) but from one 'true king'. This was most likely a separate oracle, and its juxtaposition here with that on the sheep and shepherds has resulted in a certain prophetic mixing of metaphors. The effect is to focus on the 'true king' of the house of David (who is, of course, the shepherd par excellence of the Old Testament - see 2 Sam. 7:8 ff.) all the hopes for true shepherding. This hoped-for king is the Messiah - the 'branch' (or 'shoot') of David (see Zech. 3:8; 6:12; Is. 11:1 ff.) - and the salvation he will bring is seen as covering the kingdom of Judah and that of Israel (destroyed about 721 BC). All of this, in the context of today's liturgy especially, underlies the concept of Jesus as Shepherd-Messiah.
