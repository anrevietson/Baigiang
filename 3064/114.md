# The Passover lamb

The word for "lamb" used in today's readings does not refer to the meek, helpless lamb led to slaughter, even though we may at times think of Jesus in this way. Rather, the word used here refers to the leading lamb, or ram, of the flock. The Lamb of God is primarily an image of the leadership of Jesus. He is the leader who sacrifices himself for all his followers. The ancient rite on which the Passover was based was a rite of prayer for the fruitfulness of the flock, and this is precisely what it became with the self-sacrifice of Jesus who died for the growth of the Church.
St Paul justly refers to the Lord's Supper as the establishing of a new covenant with God in Christ. John, from whose gospel our third reading is taken, begins his gospel by telling us that Jesus came from the Father full of grace and truth and that we have all been given a share of this fullness (Jn 1:16). The new covenant, established in Jesus' death, and celebrated the night before he died, is a mutual commitment between God and Jesus' disciples that they will live the dimension of love in anew way. Jesus had revealed the Father's love to us and taught us how to love each other and thus be recognised as his disciples. He taught us to love (Jn 13:15), urged us to love (Jn 13:34), and commanded us to love as he did (Jn 15:1 7).After the washing of the disciples' feet, Jesus said: "For I have given you an example, that you also should do as I have done to you" (Jn 13:15). This is our Teacher and Lord who reveals a new kind of covenant love as the focus of our lives.
The episode of the washing of the feet was a sign of Jesus' love for his disciples. St John believed such a simple gesture went to the heart of the meaning of the Eucharist. It was and is an occasion when we can reconfirm our dedication to the new covenant of Christian love, revealed by Jesus and ratified by his death. Matthew advised us never to approach this celebration unless we are at peace with everyone (Mt 5:23-24) Paul condemned anything that could weaken the rest of the love of the community for each of its members (1 Cor 13:3). Sharing the Eucharist already shows that we are reconciled to each other, but it goes on to celebrate that love is the sharing of prayer, singing, attentive reflection on the sources of our faith, and time together in the Lord's friendship. The Eucharist, however, goes even further when it is seen in the way John presents it today. It is a promise even a prophecy - of our future mutual commitment to build up the quality of love in our community.
As Catholics we believe the sacraments are efficacious in what they symbolise. The Eucharist symbolises the covenant and love between the community and God. We believe through the power of Jesus death that this sacrament not only portrays and symbolises love, but through Jesus it effects love among us.
Jesus is our Teacher and Lord; the Teacher and Lord of love.
