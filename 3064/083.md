# The Lord's Supper at Corinth

second Reading (1 Cor 11:23-26). The context of this reading is interesting (vv. 17-22,27-34). Paul is concerned with the way in which the Christian Eucharist was being celebrated. The Christian community ale a full meal together, an agape or love-feast, But Paul is distressed at the way in which the agape is being inculturated in Corinth. The Christians came from different socio-economic groups and brought different eating habits with them. There is a suggestion that each group may have brought their own food. Some evidently came from the moneyed, leisured classes. They arrived early and ate luxuriantly, as they did at home or in one another's houses the community still gathered in somebody's house, not yet in a church. They ate and drank well and showed the effects of over-indulgence. Other Christians were poor people or slaves. They arrived late. They had little food to bring or there was little food left and they were left hungry and embarrassed, Paul suggested holding the Eucharist at a time when everyone could come. If they could not wait to eat, they should eat something at home before they came. But the separation of full meal for the community and ritual celebration of the Eucharist was still in the future.
Our short liturgical reading extracts from this longer passage the account of the Lord's Supper which is being recalled on Holy Thursday. In recent tradition homilies on this text have emphasised the aspects of the Eucharist relevant to the post-Reformation controversies Prominent among these was the emphasis on the Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist. The contrast between Catholic realistic language (transubstantiation) and Protestant spiritualising accounts of the Eucharist led frequently to the false conclusion that only Catholics believed in the presence of Christ in the Eucharist. The emphasis on worthiness (v. I 7) was referred not only to the need for an orthodox belief in the Real Presence but also to the need for penance and purification before approaching the sacrament. The very strong insistence on worthiness, well-intended though it was, discouraged the majority of Catholics from frequent Communion until this century.
The perspective of Saint Paul is that Christians are sacramentally incorporated into the body of Christ by his death, Their behaviour to one another must reflect this mystery. The Eucharist is its sacramental celebration and realisation. Paul is concerned with the community and how their behaviour and relationships with one another reflect or do not reflect their incorporation into the one body of Christ. He does not invite them to decide whether or not they will go to Communion, He expects them to do so, as it makes them members of Christ's body. But he insists that they think about what they are doing when they are receiving Communion. They are to make sure that their dealings with one another correspond to the sacrament of their incorporation into the one body of Christ.
