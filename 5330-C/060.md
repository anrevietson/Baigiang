# Work and the coming of Christ

Second Reading (2 Thes. 3 :7-12). It is quite clear from the correspondence between Paul and his converts in Thessalonika that idleness was a particularly grave problem there. Paul had already drawn their attention to the need to earn their living, so that they would command the respect of outsiders, and be dependent on nobody (1 Thes. 4 :11 f.), and again in 5 :14 he advises the readers to 'admonish the idle' . The situation as reflected in the second letter shows no improvement. Paul is forced to state his message even more clearly: 'If anyone will not work, let him not eat' (3 :10). The Christian, no less than any other man, is subject to the
universal law of work. Indeed he ought to look on work as much more than an occupation he must reluctantly engage in so that his body will be sustained. It is a positive contribution to the creative work of God. God has made man as master of his creation (Gen. 1:26) in order that he might subdue it and bring it to the glorification of God (cf. Sir. 17 :3-10). According to Vatican II, man unfolds the creator's work by his labour, and contributes to the realization in history of the divine plan by his own personal industry (Church in the Modern World, no.34). To be voluntarily idle, therefore, is to remove oneself from the general movement of man's co-operation with God the creator. Paul's message is still valid: no Christian ought to sponge on others, not even under the pretext that the present world is hardly worth interesting oneself in since the Lord's coming will bring it to an end.
