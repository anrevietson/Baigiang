# Justified by faith

Second Reading (Rom. 5:1-2, 5-8). This consists of two short sections taken from a very significant text, viz. Romans 5:1 - ii. In this passage St Paul describes the power of Christian hope.
Hope can only be fully understood by those who have formerly experienced a deep sense of sinfulness, which is, let it he said, quite distinct from that psychological state which is commonly described as 'guilt complex'. In the opening chapters of the Epistle to the Romans St Paul had explained that all men, Jew and Gentile, are involved in sin and in desperate need of God's saving power (Rom. 3:23). It is impossible for a man by his own power to make the transition from sin to grace.
It is only possible for those who open their souls by faith to accept God's saving initiative. The good news of the Gospel is simply the announcement that God has indeed taken this initiative by reconciling the world with himself through the redeeming act of Christ who willingly accepted death on the cross. This led to his personal resurrection on the third day, which is the pledge that we shall all rise like him and with him at the end. The barrier which formerly separated us from God has been broken down and we have free access to God (Rom. 5:2); we are now in a state of peace with God (Rom. 5:1) and we have hope (Rom. 5:2).
The object of this hope is life with God through Christ, a reality which has been achieved in principle but not yet fully realised. As long as this world remains Christians living in it are still, in a sense, separated from the ultimate achievement of their destiny. They must now live in the hope of that ultimate achievement.
But there are abundant grounds for this hope. When we were still sinners and in desperate need that Christ should die on the cross he did just that for us. Now that we have been reconciled with God we still need Christ to lead us to the point at which our life with God will be secured. St Paul assured his readers that Christ will be even more willing to give us the support we need in our time of trial than he was to die for us in the first place when we were still sinners.
It must always be remembered that St Paul was deeply conscious of sin in the world and denounced superficial complacency. But underlying this apparently gloomy attitude was a very strong current of optimism and joy. For St Paul human nature was indeed fallen and sinful; but it was a fallen nature that had been abundantly redeemed.
