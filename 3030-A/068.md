# The Samaritan woman

The Gospel (John 4:5-42). The narrative element of this passage is vivid and easy to follow. The significance of the reading lies in Christ's words and in the measure of self-revelation which is conveyed by the combination of words and deeds. The transition from a scene of everyday life to the presentation of theological teaching occurs at verse ten; and this teaching is continued in verses thirteen and fourteen.
The evidence that Jacob gave this piece of land to his son Joseph is contained in Joshua 24:32 and in Genesis 48:33 and 33:19. The tradition that it was Jacob himself who dug the well is not attested in the Old Testament. If this 'Jacob's Well' is correctly identified with the present-day well about a quarter of a mile from the village of Balata in Samaria, it is more than 100 feet deep.
The woman sees that Christ's words as recorded in verse ten are not meaningful in the literal everyday sense, so she asks an apparently foolish question to give Jesus a chance to explain their symbolical meaning. The Jews would call a flowing spring living water as opposed to dead water, collected in a cistern or tank, with obvious preference for the former. Christ asserts that he is able and willing to give her a reality of greater value than the water which she could haul up from the well at which they were sitting. This claim to be able to give a 'living and life-giving water' recalls his claim to be the living and life-giving bread which he will make in John 6:34 The theme of Jesus as the giver of life will reappear in the third reading of the Fifth Sunday of Lent, i.e. the account of the raising of Lazarus. However, that passage treats the topic in a very different historical setting and in a very different literary form.
In today's first reading from the book of Exodus water was presented as the factor which could save people from death by thirst, and as such, was used as a vehicle of theological teaching. But here in St John's Gospel the contrast is between the fertility of a well-watered garden or oasis and the arid infertility of a waterless soil. This is intended to suggest the difference between those who receive Christ's gift and those who do not - either because it was not offered to them or because, when it was offered they did not accept it, like the woman who left her water jug behind empty (v.28).
There is no explanation here of the precise nature of this life-giving 'gift of God' but certain aspects of it are implied :
(1) This new life is incompatible with a sinful life; moral conversion is inevitably linked with spiritual conversion.
(2) This new life requires adequate worship of the Father. This could not be achieved even in God' temple in Jerusalem. (N.B. : In John, chapter three, it has already been said that the new life is given in a sort of rebirth from the activity of the Spirit, which is required for this new worship. And in chapter two Christ has already indicated that his own self will replace the temple as the meeting place of man with God.)
(3) The possession of this new life is the fulfilment of the religious destiny of Israel and the salvation of the world(Collaborators are needed to convey this gift to men which they do by leading men to faith (v.41).
The Christians for whom St John was writing knew much more about God's gift than could be presented to the Samaritan woman in a single conversation; but John considered it useful to compose this narrative in order to emphasise certain aspects of this gift which might otherwise have gone unnoticed.
