# Rejoice always

Second Reading (1 Thes. 5:16-24). This reading is taken from the concluding section of a letter in which Paul exhorts a young Christian community to a life in keeping with their calling as children of the light and children of the day. They should encourage those who are faint-~ hearted and in this way help them to become mature Christians. Faith, hope and love require this of them. In particular he exhorts to Christian joy, to prayer and thanksgiving. This joy, of which Paul speaks more than once, is a gift of the Holy Spirit (Gal. 5:22). It does not mean the absence of suffering. The Thessalonians, in fact, had had their fill of persecutions (cf. 1 Thes. 2:13-16). A characteristic of Christian joy is that it can exist with trials and sufferings; these in fact can give rise to joy since from faith believers know how these unite them with Christ. We should also note how the apostle exhorts them not to try to suppress the Spirit (RSV: 'quench the Spirit') or treat prophecy with contempt. The Spirit made his presence felt within the early community through gifts, or charisms, of various kinds. One of these charisms was prophecy, a gift highly prized by St Paul (cf. 1 Cor. 12:8-10,28-30; chapters 12-14, especially 14:1). Prophecy was not so much foretelling future events as forthright speaking within the community on the nature and obligations of Christianity. It was almost inevitable that among the charismatic there should have been a certain amount of self-deception. Not all who believed they were prophets or presented themselves as prophets were really sent by God. It may have been that a reaction to the misuse of charisms and of prophecy was leading the Church of Thessalonica to an outright rejection of both. To do so would be to forget the spiritual nature of the Church. Not that everything said or done by those who present themselves or are accepted as prophets or charismatic is to be accepted by that very fact as true or good. There is need for a discernment of spirits; everything is to be tested and what is good accepted. Paul gives no hard and fast rules for distinguishing true manifestations of the Spirit from the false. Nor are criteria in this field easy. The truths of faith constitute one. For the rest the good sense of Christians has to be relied on. In this they will be guided by the nature of the Church as the new community endowed with the Spirit. An easy life and the maintenance of the status quo would scarcely be a yardstick to meet with Paul's approval.
