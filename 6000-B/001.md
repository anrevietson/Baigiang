# The visit of the Magi

The Gospel (Mat. 2:1-12). Among all the episodes that go to make up the story of the birth of Jesus as told by Matthew and Luke, Matthew's Magi narrative is one that has never failed to exercise the ingenuity of scholars. Today, most scholars would consider it imprudent to adopt the attitude of earlier liberal scholars who dismissed the story as purely legendary. On the other hand most would agree that Matthew's interest in the episode was not' primarily historical but theological. All would agree that the author relied heavily on Old Testament ideas and imagery to get across the point that the King Messiah who had been expected by Israel had come in the person of Jesus, that he had been rejected by the Jews and accepted by the Gentiles.
The birth of the child in Bethlehem, the home of David (1 Sam. 16), would have suggested that he belonged to the family of David. This point would have been of interest to the Jews who expected their Messiah to come from that family. The author emphasises the royal dignity and messianic stature of the child by applying to him the messianic prophecy (Micah 5:1-5) which stated that the awaited Messiah would come from Bethlehem. The idea of a king of the Jews being born in Bethlehem of Judea may contain an allusion to the messianism of Genesis 49:10 - 'The sceptre shall not pass from Judah.' Although Matthew does not explicitly refer to Numbers 24:17 ('a star shall come forth from Jacob'), that text most probably influenced him in his description of the star which was seen by the Magi. That verse from Numbers was interpreted in a messianic sense in Qumran and it is probable that Matthew's readers would also have seen in it a reference to the David Messiah.
Since many legends circulated at the time of Christ about famous men whose birth was marked by the appearance of a star, some scholars are inclined to relegate Matthew's story of the star to the realms of the legendary. But the Magi were astrologers from Babylonia where Jewish ideas about an expected prince of salvation were probably known even to the pagans. The Magi could have connected some astronomical phenomenon with the coming of the Jewish saviour and they could have set out to find him. It seems impossible to identify that particular phenomenon, but that is hardly a sufficient reason for rejecting the story as non-historical.
Whatever may be said about the historical details of the story this passage effectively shows how little the coming of Jesus corresponded to the Jewish messianic expectations. The absence of Jewish dignitaries and the presence of Gentile princes at Christ's birth shows how God's epiphany )or revelation began to extend beyond the confines of Judaism and to embrace the whole human race.
