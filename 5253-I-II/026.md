# The sending of the seventy-two

Gospel (Lk 10:1-12.17-20). The tribes of Israel were traditionally regarded as having been twelve; the number of the gentile (non-Jewish) nations as seventy or seventy-two (going on the number in the table of nations in Genesis chapter 10 in the original Hebrew or Greek Septuagint translation). Luke records two missions on Jesus' part, one of the Twelve (Lk 9:1-6), another (here) of the seventy or seventy-two disciples (it is uncertain which of the two numbers is the original). What Jesus said 10 the Twelve about mission held good in the early Church for the mission to larger groups, in fact to the entire world. The reading in today's gospel puts before us the early Church's reflection and meditation on mission. It shows us how deeply convinced these early Christians were of the need for mission. A community that is not missionary minded, that is not fired with zeal for spreading the gospel message, is not truly Christian. A sense of mission means living in one's person the reality of the mission of Christ, fired with a sense of purpose. intent on getting on with the work to be done, not burdened with unnecessary baggage or wasting time along the way. It means being bearers of the message of peace and reconciliation, while at the same time being prepared for rejection. Living examples of what Luke means by missionaries, of course, are the pair ('in pairs')Paul and Barnabas as described in Luke's other volume, the Acts of the Apostles.
A message stressed both in the second reading and in the gospel is the serious consequences of rejecting the gospel message. God's plan will not be impeded by infidelity on the part of any individual, group or nation It is easier lose the faith than regain it. In the developed world today loss of faith, abandonment of the faith, is commonplace. In our thinking such a process might even come to be regarded as inevitable. Saint Patrick is believed to have prayed that he would never lose those he had won to faith in Christ. Let us pray and work that his desire come time.
Another point worth bearing in mind is that we are now in the last decade of the second millennium, a decade dedicated to evangelisation in the different Christian churches. What Luke has to say on preaching the Good News in today's Gospel reading could well serve as a basis for reflection on what we should be doing today, individually and as a community, to bring Christ's saving message to the men and women of our own time. Saint Patrick came to freehand twice: the second time he came of his own free will, but the first time he was brought there by force. What must it have felt like to arrive in a foreign land, knowing that you are no longer free to come and go as you wish. Yet it was through this experience of slavery, Patrick tells us himself, that he came to know the life of prayer and was enabled to develop inner freedom. The years of captivity were for Patrick not time wasted and to be forgotten, but rather years of hidden growth and development, preparing him for that moment when the Lord would set him free. And set him free God did.
According to Patrick, it was the Lord who helped him to escape but, as we acknowledge gratefully today, he did so in order that Patrick might return to freehand, this time of his own free will. Patrick was a reluctant immigrant once only; the second time he arrived in Ireland was very different from the first. What changed his attitude, undoubtedly, was the experience of his first stay, during which he came to know the Lord in prayer and came to know the fresh too, whose voice he would later hear, calling him to return.
Many of us, perhaps, are reluctant servants of God, and of each other; deep down in our heart we want to be free, to getaway from obligations that bind us. We're a bit like Patrick the slave, or like Jeremiah in today's first reading who tries to tell the Lord, 'Don't choose me; choose someone else instead.' The Lord invites us to go beyond the experience of slavery and to develop inner freedom, choosing freely to make God's mission our own. Then we can be like the mature Patrick, working hand-in-hand with Christ, being attentive to his presence and co-operating with him in building up that kingdom of God which is very near.
We are chosen by God and sent by him as Patrick was, and as the apostles were before him: eho~n to share the fullness of God's life, to experience the Passover from captivity to freedom, discovering that we are no longer slaves, but sons and daughters of God; chosen, however, in order to be sent. When we truly experience that Passover freedom, then we realise that this precious gift of God is given to us not for hoarding, since that too would enslave us, but for sharing. The good news can only be brought to the poor by those who have themselves been enriched by it, and those riches of the gospel are given to us so that we might share them with others.
