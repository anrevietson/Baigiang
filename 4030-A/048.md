# Peter's Pentecost discourse

First Reading (Acts 2:14, 22-28). This excerpt - part of Peter's Pentecostal discourse - relates the essential points in all the early apostolic preaching. Called the 'kerygma' (literally, a herald's pronouncement), this early preaching was in the nature of a plain statement of the facts of the Christian message concerning which there was complete and total agreement. These were - Jesus lived as a man approved by God, he worked miracles, healed the sick and did good to all; the Jews had him put to death, but God raised him from the dead; the apostles were witnesses of this, and scripture proved it.
The following points in our reading deserve mention :
(1) The crucifixion and death of Christ was no accident; it was 'foreseen and provided for' in the eternal plan of God. Neither was it some kind of emergency measure which God adopted when everything else had failed. It was instead a 'window in time to allow us to see the suffering love which is eternally in the heart of God'. If the Old Testament indicated clearly that God loved man, the life and death of Christ showed how much he loved him. We should not then look on Christ's death as something that helped change God's attitude to man from one of condemnation to one of love. This is not true. God sent his Son into the world because he loved it so much!
(2) The centrality and importance of the resurrection in the early kerygma can hardly be overvalued. The Acts of the Apostles has been called the Gospel of the Resurrection. The crucifixion and death of Jesus is viewed as only a prelude to the mighty action of God in raising him up And the effects of the resurrection on the Christian community can be visibly seen and measured. Peter, the preacher of the Pentecostal sermon, has come a long way from the day when, at Jesus' prediction of his own future suffering, he began to rebuke him (Mark 8:32). Such is the power of the resurrection and the giving of the Spirit. The death of Christ had bewildered the apostles, shattered their dreams and plunged their hopes into despair. But the resurrection dramatically changed all that. Where there was cowardice and despair, now there was courage and hope; where once there was an inability to penetrate and appreciate the mystery of Christ's sufferings and death, now there was a Spirit-given insight into and acceptance of them.
(3) One is reminded of the musical Camelot as one tries to describe the spirit that animated the early Christians in the wake of Jesus' resurrection and the coming of the Spirit. Rooted in pagan myth and legend Camelot is, however, in many ways 'unassailably Catholic'. For it is a happy place : banners wave gaily in the brisk breeze, it is peopled with courtly men and women, and echoes to the strains of music, laughter and song. But it is a realistic place too. Its people know they will suffer. But they do not fear. It is a place of vision : the knowledge of future trouble and suffering does not dissolve the vision in their hearts. And the most noble men among them will go off in search of the Holy Grail.
We can do with a draught of the spirit of Camelot. For it is in essence the spirit of the risen Christ. And we are a resurrection people!
