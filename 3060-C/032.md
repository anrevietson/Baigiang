# The Passion according to Luke

Gospel (Lk 22:14-23:56). The general outline of Luke's account of the passion follows the traditional synoptic account, originally based on Mark. Luke adds seven specific elements to the story. Luke moves the dispute between the apostles regarding who is greatest from Jesus' journey (Mk 10:
4244) to when the apostles were at table for the Last Supper (Lk22:24-27) He inserts during the meal a final promise from Jesus that his close disciples will receive the gift of entrance into the kingdom (22:28-30). Luke understands the kingdom as coming with Jesus' death and resurrection, and now the kingdom seems closer than ever. Luke describes a coming time of crisis and urges the apostles to be ready (22:35-38). In the Gethsemane scene Luke adds the sweating of blood as an indication of the intensity of Jesus' sufferings but also includes the appearance of a consoling angel as an assurance of God's response to the Lord's prayer (22:4344). The added information that Jesus was sent to Herod Antipas (23:4-12), the ruler of Galilee, is expanded so that Luke transfers to Herod's soldiers the mocking of Jesus that other writers attribute to the Romans (Mk 15:16-20). In line with his usual tendency to show women exemplifying basic qualities of Christian life, Luke gives us the story of the meeting of Jesus with the women of Jerusalem, representatives of the Jewish people who supported Jesus and mourned for him (23:27-32). Luke's final addition is that of an expanded version of the three temptations on the cross, paralleling the three temptations in the wilderness at the beginning of the ministry.
Luke edits his sources to give four emphases that pick up focuses of the rest of his narrative. He takes blame away from the Romans and sees the leaders of the Jews as responsible for the death of Jesus. Thus, Pilate finds Jesus innocent and is reluctant to put him to death (22:20-25), but Herod's soldiers mock him. Luke blames the Jewish leaders for Jesus' death (13:31-32) possibly because by the time he is writing the gospel he is interested in developing a good relationship between the Church and the State. Luke stresses the necessity of Jesus' suffering as part of Gods plan (13:33; 22:53; 24:26). Unlike Paul, Luke does not attach direct saving significance to Jesus' suffering but sees that it is the whole self-gift of Jesus - incarnation, life, ministry, suffering, passion, death, resurrection, Ascension, and privileged place at the right hand of the Father that makes Jesus the author of life for the world (Acts 3:15).
