# The tradition of the Lord's Supper

Second Reading (1 Cor. 11:23-26). In his letter to the Corinthians Paul is drawing his readers back from the un-Christian behaviour which they had been practising at their Eucharists to the true tradition he had himself received. Paul's chief point is that the Eucharist proclaims the death of the Lord in such a way that practical failure to recognize this involves contempt for the death of Christ. The striking point is that Paul does not think of the Eucharist and Christ's presence through it in a static Way, as might be suggested by the formulas 'This is . '. Instead, the account is full of dynamic expressions. It is no mere making present of Christ's body and blood, but it is a proclamation, and that of his death, so not of something physical but of an event. Similarly the cup is 'the covenant in my blood', that is, an event, the making of a covenant, which has lasting and definitive consequences for the life of the people who are included in the covenant. This is emphasized still more by two more phrases: 'as a memorial of me' and 'until the Lord comes'. The former of these recalls the prayer of the Jewish paschal meal that God may remember the Messiah, that is that he may remember his promise to send the Messiah, that his kingdom may come. The 'memorial' is, therefore, not of something in the past, but is a memorial of what is to happen in the future, and links to the latter phrase mentioned, 'until the Lord comes'. Prominent, therefore is the idea that the Eucharist not only looks back to the past but also looks forward to the fulfilment of the kingdom in the second coming of Christ, and is a reminder that it is to come. This is the reason for the lesson of Paul's teaching, that the Eucharist must be celebrated in a way befitting the expectation of the second coming, since each celebration of the Eucharist should by its memorial further and bring nearer the fulfilment of the kingdom.
