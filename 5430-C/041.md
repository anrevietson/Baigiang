# The tradition of the Lord's Supper

Second Reading (1 Cor. 11:23-26). In his letter to' the Corinthians Paul is drawing his readers hack from the un-Christian behaviour which they had been practising at their Eucharists to the true tradition he had himself received. He breaks into a style 'and vocabulary which' are foreign to him, to' give what is already in the year AD 55 apparently a piece of ancient tradition learnt by heart by new converts. This is the earliest literary account' of the institution which we possess, probably representing that current in the Greek-speaking communities, as opposed to the very similar version given by Mark and Matthew, which 'suggest transmission through the Aramaic speaking communities of Syria and Palestine.
Paul's' chief point is that the 'Eucharist proclaims the death of the Lord in such a way that practical failure to recognise this involves 'contempt for the death of Christ. The striking point is that. Paul and does not think of the Eucharist and Christ's presence through it in a static way,, as might be suggested by the formulas 'This is ' Instead, the acc6unt is full of dynamic expressions. It is no mere making present of Christ's body and blood, but it is a proclamation, and that of his death, so not of something physical but of an event. Similarly the cup is 'the covenant in my blood', that is,, an event, the making of a Covenant, which has lasting and definitive consequences for the life of the people who are included in the covenant. This is emphasised still more by two more phrases : 'as a memorial of me' and 'until the Lord comes',. The former of these recalls the prayer of the Jewish paschal meal that God may remember the Messiah, that is that he may remember his promise to send the Messiah, that his kingdom may come. The 'memorial' is, therefore, not of something in the past, but is a memorial of what is to happen in the future, and links to the latter phrase mentioned, 'until the Lord comes'. Prominent, therefore, is the idea that the Eucharist not only looks back to the past but also looks forward to the fulfilment of the kingdom in the second coming of Christ, and is a reminder that it is to come. This is the reason for the lesson of Paul's teaching, that the Eucharist must be celebrated in a way befitting the expectation of the second coming, since each celebration of the Eucharist should' by its memorial further and bring nearer the fulfilment of the kingdom.
From Jesus' words we also gain a precious insight into his own interpretation of his approaching passion. Firstly he sees it in terms of the prophecy of the suffering servant of the Lord in Isaiah 53. This mysterious figure was to suffer for the sins of others voluntarily and so reconcile theorem to God, passing through humiliation, mockery and agony to reach a final paradoxical vindication by God which at the same time brought him recognition by men. This personality figures clearly and prominently in Jesus' thoughts, as we see in many of his sayings about the passion whose wording is reminiscent of the prophecy. In the Mark-Matthew version of the last supper it is reflected in the words 'my blood . . . which is to be poured out for many', though it has become less clear in Paul's 'which is for you', though this still contains the idea of vicarious suffering which is the keynote of the prophecy. Secondly, Jesus sees and expresses his passion in terms of a covenant with God by which Israel became God's people, so the new past in which Jesus' is the victim 'rescues man 'from slavery to sin and' forms the people of God of the new covenant.
