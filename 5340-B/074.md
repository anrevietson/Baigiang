# The Son of Man

First Reading (Dan. 7 :13-14). The mysterious figure of the Son of Man who is introduced in today's first reading appears for the first time in the Bible in the rather strange vision described in chapter seven of the book of Daniel from which today's passage is taken. In his vision Daniel saw four beasts which represented four great pagan empires which had in the course of centuries influenced Israel's history. In the vision the Babylonians were symbolised by the lion (v.4), the Medes by the bear (v.5) and the Persians by the leopard (v.6). The Greeks whose oppressive rule the author himself had experienced were represented by a beast too dreadful to be compared with any known ammal (v.7). The point the author wishes to make is that when the last of the four beasts is destroyed, that is, when the Greeks, the last of the four empires, are overthrown, the Jews will rule over all the nations. OF, as the author puts it: The saints of the most high shall receive the kingdom, and possess the kingdom for ever' (v.18, cf. v.27).
It is in the context of this vision and its explanation that the 'one like a son of man' referred to in today's reading must be understood. Whereas the four beasts came up out of the sea (v.3), that is' from the regions of the forces of evil, the 'one like a son of man' came on the clouds of heaven' (v.13), that is, from God. While the beasts represent the pagan kingdoms the 'one like a son of man' represents the saints of the most high, or the people of Israel, who receive sovereignty over the nations from God. It follows then that the 'one like a son of man' is not an individual and therefore not the personal Messiah whom the Jews expected to inaugurate the new and glorious kingdom of Israel (cf. e.g. Is. 9 :6 f.; 11:1-5; P55. 2; 110). Later Jewish literature regarded the 'one like a son of man' as an individual, and it is possible that at the time of Christ the term 'Son of Man' was considered a messianic title, at least among some marginal groups.
Whatever may be said of the title 'Son of Man' among the Jews we know that Jesus frequently used it when referring to himself, to his ministry through which he inaugurated the kingdom of God and to his future coming in glory (cf. e.g. Mt. 13 :37; Mark 2 :10, 28; Luke 7 :34; 12 :40). But Jesus gave new and added meaning to the old title. The 'Son of Man' as described by Daniel was glorious and victorious and the text in no way suggests that he would have to endure suffering and humiliation. Jesus, however, knew that he could establish the kingdom of God and reach the glory of the Son of Man only by following the path of the Suffering Servant of Yahweh (cf. Is. 52 :13 - 53 :12). The Son of Man had to suffer many things before he could enter into his glory (cf. Mark 8:31; luke 24:26; John 8:28). It was the Father's will that he should be rejected by men, and even done to death by them, before 'sovereignty, glory and kingship' were conferred on him.
The Responsorial Psalm (Ps. 92 :1-2, 5). This psalm which celebrates the majesty and might of Yahweh the glorious king of the chosen people is very fittingly selected for use by the new people of God who today give glory to Christ their King and Lord. Like the Jews of old we praise our King and our God as creator ('the world you make firm'), as our benevolent lawgiver ('your decrees are to be trusted') and we accept the demands that he makes on us whom he has admitted into his household ('holiness is fitting to your house') .
