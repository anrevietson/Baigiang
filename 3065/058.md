# The suffering servant

First Reading (Is 52:13-53:12). Second Isaiah, written after the collapse of Judah and period of exile, is sombre in tone. The community re thinks its faith and begins to see in Cyrus of Persia array of hope for its own liberation. The book contains the Servant Songs (Is 42:1-4; 49:1-6; 50:4-9; 52:13-53:12), with today's reading being the fourth song that focuses on the sufferings of the righteous for the sake of others. In biblical times, various understandings of the servant's identity emerged, such as historical figures, heirs to the throne, the prophet, or Israel itself. The suffering servant as Israel, suffering for others and triumphant in the end, was a favoured interpretation. Within the Christian context, the song certainly points to Jesus whose death for us brings salvation and life.
In Isaiah 52:13 and 53: 10-l2 God speaks, announcing the transformation of the servant from humiliation to glorification. The centre section reminds readers of various scripture passages including Ezekiel 4:1-8, that records one bearing the sins of others, and Leviticus 16, focusing on the scapegoat, sacrifice and rite of atonement. Theologically, the songs represent a change from the long-standing tradition of suffering as punishment for sin. Here, the suffering and sacrifice are for the sins of others and, in this, the servant is a model as his goodness, attitudes, silence, and acceptance become the example for the community. The Christian tradition sees in the servant, Jesus, who in his suffering and death becomes the model for us and a sign to the nations. The early Church remembered this handing over of the Son in its writings (Mt 17:22; Jn 8:28-30, 35; Ac 3:13; 1 Cor 11:23), and in its proclamation, and this emphasis is an important part of the Good Friday Liturgy.
