# Our compassionate high priest

Second Reading (Heb. 4:14-16; 5:7-9). The author of Hebrews presents Jesus as our faithful and compassionate high priest. The passage 5:1-10 shows that Christ has perfectly met the requirements of priesthood : he is a man (by the incarnation) officially instituted as a mediator between God and men, who pleads the cause of men before God, and who offers the gifts of men to God, especially sacrifice for sin. Because a true priest must be compassionate, showing great indulgence and benevolence to sinners, Jesus possesses, in an eminent degree, this fundamental quality of the priest. The verses seven to ten present Jesus in an attitude of suppliant before the Father - Gethsemane is manifestly in mind.
We are shown the means (suffering) by which the saving work of Jesus is effected (7 f.), as well as the result for himself and for those who trust in him (9 f.). Through his obedience he gained an enriching psychological experience, a practical understanding and appreciation of suffering which would enable him fully to sympathise with his brethren. Moreover, our high priest has taken his proper place in the very presence of God - and through him we have access to the mercy of God. He has entered heaven, but he is joined to us still in his perfect understanding of our trials and difficulties. The distance between us, abolished by the incarnation, has not been broadened again by the ascension; he is always ready and able to help us because he is always our compassionate high priest (4:14-16).
The Passion (John 18:1-19:42). The passion according to john is highly dramatic in that the evangelist presents the passion as a triumph of the Son of God. We can hope to do no more here than give some indication of this. Throughout John's passion account, Jesus is always serenely in command. At his arrest, his adversaries are prostrate before his majesty (18:6), illustrating what he himself had declared : 'No one takes my life from me, but I lay it down of my own accord' (10:18). He is master of his own fate. The central section of the narrative is the trial before Pilate (18:28-19:16). The theme recurring throughout the section is the kingship of Christ. When Pilate asks, 'Are you the king of the Jews?', Jesus calmly claims that he is, though his kingship is not of this world. Rather, he reigns in men's hearts by truth - that is, by the revelation that he brings. 'The Jews' (the leaders who see Jesus as a danger to them, representing 'the establishment', and who are determined to destroy him) reject this king. He cannot be ignored :
scourged, crowned with thorns, arrayed in a purple robe, he is saluted as 'king of the Jews'. Pilate's 'Behold the man ! ' is a note of Johannine irony. Pilate is saying : you have nothing to fear from this helpless man; but John is seeing the Son of Man, the judge of the world. All the while, it is Pilate who is being judged. Although he has recognised and three times acknowledges the innocence of Jesus (18:38; 19:4, 6), he ends by yielding to political blackmail. He is a man who will not make a decision for or against Jesus -and finds himself trapped. And the Jews, too, are being judged. 'Here is your king', Pilate tells them (19:4); 'We have no king but Caesar', they reply (19:15). The real trial is over. For, in the presence of Jesus, 'the Jews' have judged themselves; they have spoken their own sentence.
The way of the cross becomes a kind of triumphal procession. In contrast with the synoptic accounts, here is no Simon of Cyrene
Jesus is the master of his own destiny. Already in John's Gospel, the cross had been shown as the throne of this king : 'I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all men to myself' (12:32). And here, the passion ends with a cry of triumph : 'It is fulfilled' (19:30). It follows that the story cannot end with the death of Jesus, for John sees the deeper reality symbolised by the crucifixion. The death-and-resurrection comprise one complete event; for this reason he characterises the death of Jesus as his 'glorification'. And so, his solemn attestation to the flow of water and blood from the side of the dead Christ takes on. its significance : blood and water stand for Eucharist and Baptism - the life that Christ gives. Now the Spirit is given because Jesus is glorified (cf. 7:38 f.).
