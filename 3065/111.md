# The Passion according to John

Passion (Jn 18:1-19:42). John's account of the passion contains the following episodes: the arrest in Gethsemane (18:112), the appearance before Annas and Caiaphas and Peter's denials (18:13-27), the Trial before Pilate (18:28-9:16), the crucifixion (19:17-30), a final testimony (19:31-37), and the burial (19:3842). While the gospel has similarities to the Synoptics, particularly Mark, the passion narrative is probably based on an independent tradition.
John prepares the reader for the passion in his use of 'the hour' throughout the Book of Signs (2:4; 7:6.8.30; 8:20; 12:23), and through the biblical image of being 'lifted up' (3:14; 8:28; 12:31). As the passion unfolds, the reader becomes aware of John's emphasis on Jesus' suffering and death in light of his glorification. The Book of Glory includes events of the last days, the passion, death, and resurrection of the Lord (Jn 13-20). The image of majesty predominates in the narrative and absent are such familiar episodes as the agony in the garden and mocking of Jesus on the cross. Jesus is king of the Jews and Passover lamb slain for our sins. Throughout the text, the author sees the suffering and death as the glorification of the Lord - a more profound insight into this mystery than that of the Synoptic writers who see glory following the passion.
In the early sections of the passion narrative, Jesus responds to the question of the guards by saying 'I am he.' This phase, 'l am,' is a known translation of the name of Yahweh and, in its use, Jesus suggests a functional relationship to God. The incident of the servant's ear, mentioned by all four evangelists, elicits Jesus' words regarding 'the cup that the Father has given me.' Then the narrative quickly recounts Peter's denial. In contrast to Jesus acknowledgement, 'l am he,' Peter says, 'l am not,' to an inquiry regarding discipleship. Acceptance and denial form a vivid contrast between teacher and follower.
The major scene of Jesus before Pilate is also a contrast: Pilate's hesitancy and attempt at neutrality on the one hand, and Jesus' calm possession of himself and decisiveness on the other. Pilate moves inside and outside the praetorium, while Jesus speaks simply of kingship and truth. Eventually Pilate decides against Jesus but places a statement above the cross, Jesus of Nazareth, king of the Jews.' fronic symbols of kingship -clothing, colours, crown of thorns - indicate Jesus' true identity.
The crucifixion itself emphasises Jesus' kingship, majesty and fulfilment of scripture. The scene is also an opportunity for John's theological reflection. At the foot of the cross, Mary and the beloved disciple represent the believing community, the Church. Jesus' offering gives birth to a new reality that will be the sign of his continued presence in the world. In John's use of scripture in this section, he draws on symbols and words used elsewhere in the gospel. Jesus' comment 'l am thirsty' suggests water and living water, vivid gospel imagery (4:14; 7:19-38). 'It is accomplished shows the completion of the Father's work and reminds hearers of the union of Father and Son. The cross itself becomes the symbol that, when 'lifted up,' gives life to the world (12:31). After the sacrifice of the lamb, Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus remove the body, wrap it with spices in linen, and lay it in a new tomb, thereby giving Jesus a royal burial. The Passover of the Lord is now complete.
