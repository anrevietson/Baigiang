# The martyrdom of the seven brothers

First Reading (2 Macc 7:1-2,9-14). Around the year 124 B.C. the author of Second Maccabees known as the Epitomist) condensed the five-volume work of Jason of Cyrene (see 2 Macc 2:23), a rhetorical history of the events surrounding the Maccabean revolt (a period from cir. 180 to 160 B.C.) In turn, the Epitomist sought to make this material relevant for Jews living in Egypt. He was bent upon reminding them of their national identity and encouraging their religious fidelity.
The Epitomist drew upon the biblical tradition of the Suffering Servant (see Is 52:13-53:12; note also Wis 1-6; Dan 12:2). This tradition clearly taught that suffering and persecution do not necessarily imply God's displeasure. Against the background of Antiochus IV Epiphanies' attack on the Jews, the Epitomist stressed the virtues of steadfastness and religious loyalty. Specifically, God will vindicate the Maccabean martyrs through resurrection. The death of the brothers is ultimately victory for Israel and judgment for the forces of Antiochus IV Epiphanies.
Whereas the author of First Maccabees spoke of Mattathias and his five sons (see 1 Macc 2:1-5), the Epitomist relates the experience of a mother and her seven sons. Because of their tenacious dedication to the faith of Israel these sons are willing to submit to the most barbarous forms of punishment. To underline such faith, the Epitomist accentuates the motivation of each son. The first son chooses dying over sinning. The second son attests that God will raise up the righteous. The third son professes that God will thoroughly restore their bodies. The fourth son acknowledges that the wicked cannot hope for resurrection. By means of resurrection the just are vindicated.
