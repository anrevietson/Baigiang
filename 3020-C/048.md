# Our citizenship is in heaven

Second Reading (Phil. 3:17-4:1). In the preceding passage (3:2-3) Paul has put the Church at Philippi on its guard against Jews or Jewish Christians (false brethren he calls them elsewhere) whom he fears would come preaching that circumcision and the ritual elements of the law of Moses (such as abstaining from certain kinds of food) were obligatory on Christians. This fact has led some scholars to believe that these same Jews are the enemies of the cross of Christ of whom he speaks in the passage read today.
More probably, however Paul has passed on from reference to the Jews to speak of the immoral lives of certain ('many' in fact) Christians who ignored the obligations of their religion. They may have culpably misinterpreted Paul's own teaching on Christian liberty. But for whatsoever reason, like some Christians in Rome (Rom. 14:17, 16:18; cf. 1 Cor. 8:8), their minds were set on the things of this earth, on eating, drinking and licentious behaviour. Even the early Church was not without its faults, despite the special graces it received! Christianity is indeed liberty, but freedom to live the Christian life, to come to know Christ and the power of his resurrection; the power to conquer sin and live in Christ which was given by the Spirit of the risen Saviour; to live a life in God's friendship that leads to the resurrection from the dead (Phil. 3:10-11). Christians share in Christ's resurrection through baptism. They already have eternal life and are, so to speak, already with Christ in the heavenly realms (Col. 3:1-3). Their true home, their fatherland or 'commonwealth' (RSV) is heaven; not merely as an object of hope but in a certain real sense here and now. This their faith should govern. their thinking; their thoughts should be centred on heaven. Through the holy Spirit the power of the risen Lord is already at work in Christians changing them into his own image, 'from one degree of glory to another' (2 Cor. 3:18). This process will be completed at the general resurrection and the second coming of Christ when man's weak earthly body will be transformed (1 Cor. 15:35-58). Christians, then, have a dual citizenship. As citizens of heaven they should perform their duties as citizens by a life worthy of the gospel (Phil. 1:27), setting their minds on their true fatherland. This does not mean withdrawal from one's duties as citizens of this world, as members of the human race. Man has the obligation of labouring for a better world, and this even by virtue of the religion he professes. The believer, however, must see this world in perspective and so use temporal things as not to lose things eternal. This outward and forward glance is, in fact, in the best interests of the earthly city. Looking on created realities as all that matter leads to sin and the ultimate disruption of the social order.
