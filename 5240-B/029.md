# Who do you say I am?

The Gospel (Mark 8 :27-35). We saw last Sunday (a purpose of the healing of the deaf-mute) that Jesus enforced silence on the beneficiaries and the witnesses of his miracles in order to avoid a mistaken identity. We find the same phenomenon in today's Gospel. The disciples recognise Jesus' Messiah ship, but their idea and Jesus' own idea of this Messiah ship do not coincide. Jesus tells them, therefore, to keep quiet about their discovery.
Jesus now proceeds to teach his disciples the kind of Messiah he 15 meant to be. Despite all that the Bible says about the value of suffering and the certain vindication of the suffering just man, the idea of a suffering Messiah was totally alien to Jesus' contemporaries. His disciples needed, therefore, to be taught about the suffering Messiah. Jesus himself must have been the first to learn this lesson (Is. 50 :5), as he reflected on his experience in the light of the Servant Songs and the psalms of the suffering just man. The reference to his future suffering and death is not so much a prophecy of the future as the necessary inference from his present situation. Similarly, his resurrection is the inevitable outcome for the just man who has put all his trust in God.
Peter's reaction to Jesus' teaching (v.32) shows that the idea of a suffering Messiah was not only alien but also totally unacceptable to Jesus' contemporaries. Together with Jesus' reply (v.33) it also illustrates how little the disciples understood Jesus' true identity. Jesus' suffering Messiah ship is not merely the object of faith, it is also to be shared. The Christian is called not only to accept Jesus as the suffering Messiah, but also to suffer with him, that is, to understand life and death as he did. This is what 'following' Jesus really means. As the Servant of Deutero-Isaiah, Jesus learns to see opposition, persecution, suffering and death as part of God's plan. The Christian must do the same, only he has the added advantage of Jesus' own example to follow.
