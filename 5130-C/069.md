# Freedom in Christ

Second Reading (Gal. 5:1, 13-18). The passage from the letter to the Galatians has reached the exhortatory part of the letter, after the doctrinal part which has been read for the previous weeks ; Paul is indicating the implications of life in Christ through the Spirit which marks the Christian. He arrives at the difficult question of the Christian and the law. The whole bent of the letter so far has been the freedom of Christians from law, their settings free through Christ from the domination and enslavement of the old law of Judaism. But then the question arises whether they are now free to do anything at all, and whether to a Christian all things are permissible, with total libertinism. This seems to have been the attitude of some Corinthian Christians, and was also an accusation levelled against Paul's teaching. His real teaching, however, is that though the Christian remains free of law he is nevertheless guided by the Spirit ; it is an internal guide not an external one, a voluntary one, a voluntary guide not. an inhibiting one, a guide which draws him on of his own volition rather than curbing and repressing his desires. The Spirit which; as he has explained in the reading of (he previous Sundays, is the new life-principle of the Christian, guides him spontaneously into its own ways, ways which are primarily dictated by and flow from love.
The question may well be asked then, why it is necessary to detail and recommend such conduct if it is, in any case, the spontaneous result of the new Christian life-principle. Paul himself will admit in the letter to the Romans, 'I fail to carry out the things I want to do, and I find myself doing the very things I hate' (7:15). Taking on the life of the Spirit, or conversion, is a gradual process, so that the Christian is transformed only gradually, and always has within himself the struggle between the Spirit and the old nature which dominated before his conversion.
