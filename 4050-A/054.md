# The choice of the Seven

First Reading (Acts 6:1-7). These verses not only begin a new chapter in Acts but mark the beginning of a new chapter in the development of the early Church. The first five chapters have dealt with the Church in Jerusalem; the incident recorded i~ today's reading sets the scene for the first missionary activity outside Jerusalem. The occasion was a complaint made by the Hellenists against the Hebrews. The Hellenists were Greek-speaking Jews who had their own synagogues where the Old Testament was read in Greek. The Hebrews were native Aramaic-speaking Jews in whose synagogues the Old Testament was read in Hebrew. The incident shows that the picture of fraternal harmony given earlier in Acts (2:42-47 and 4:32-35) was an ideal one, not a description of fact, or, at least, it did not last long. The cause of the dissension was allegedly that the Hellenist widows were not getting their share of the food and money that had been donated to help the
needy members. Possibly until now the apostles had supervised this distribution personally but with the constantly increasing numbers it would interfere too much with their proper task - presiding at the prayer of the community and explaining the good news at their liturgical gatherings. Spreading or explaining the Christian message was a service (cf. Luke 1:2). The function of the seven will be service also, although they are not called deacons in Acts. The number seven had special significance not only in the Bible but in pagan cultures as well. In the Bible it denotes totality, fullness or completeness. It seems that the seven were chosen for material ministrations but the qualities required in them are hardly needed for this purpose and, moreover, we soon find two of them preaching, working miracles and baptising. Possibly the qualities required
- good reputation, filled with the spirit and with wisdom - are mentioned in view of this later activity. The Church still requires similar qualities in those appointed to the ministry. The apostles dedicate the chosen seven to their work by prayer and the imposition of hands. This gesture affirms identity; it is an expression of solidarity between those who impose hands and those on whom they are imposed. The symbolism seems to be the communication of power and authority. It was an ancient rite (cf. Deut. 34:9). Verse seven gives a minor summary. Summaries are scattered throughout Acts. They note the continual increase in the number of Christians and here the important fact is related that many of the priests, leaders of the people, were converted to Christianity.
This passage from Acts shows that the early Church readily developed its structure when the need arose, while maintaining the authority of the twelve. From it we learn, too, that the proper function of the hierarchy is preaching and presiding at prayer, especially the eucharistic celebration, not administration of material affairs. It also makes clear that active charity and care of the poor is basic to Christianity.
The Responsorial Psalm (Ps. 33[32]:1-2, 4-5, 18-19) is part of a hymn of praise which tells us of God's providence and his care for those who trust in him. The first reading today gives one example of how God makes provision for the needy.
