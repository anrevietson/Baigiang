# The grain of wheat

Gospel (Jn 12:20-33). The second reading with its mention of the prayer and entreaties offered by Jesus must be inspired by the account of Jesus' Gethsemane experience. So too is this reading from John's gospel in which Jesus says that he is troubled and prays to be saved from his hour of agony. He knew, however, that it was in the Father's plan that he go through all this. He, too, had to be as the grain of wheat before new life would emerge. He had to have the experience of being bruised and broken, so that we would all realise that acquiring possession of the prophecy of the new covenant, of the new heart and the new spirit, is no easy matter. Through all this Jesus will have, and be seen to have, a bruised and compassionate heart. He tells us that this also is the law for his followers. They, too, must die. In their hearts they must experience what human weakness is if they are to become compassionate.
At the end of these reflections we may ask what the readings we have considered have to say to us today. They seem extremely relevant. From many parts of the world we have evidence of the terrible effects of hatred, memories of real or imagined past oppressions and present grievances. The desire for revenge is deeply rooted in the human heart. The gentle voices calling on us to recognise the destructive power of hatred and revenge are also potent. Many will be familiar with Mrs Tancred's prayer in Sean O'Casey's Juno and the Paycock, on hearing of her son's death during the Irish civil war: 'Me home is gone now. He was me only child
0 Blessed Virgin, where were you when me darling' son was riddled with bullets, when me darling' son was riddled with bullets! ... Sacred Heart of the Crucified Jesus, take away our hearts 0' stone... an' give us hearts of flesh! ... Take away this murdering' hate... an' give us Thine own eternal love!'. Here we have a prayer from the heart that must be repeated over and over again. Create for us a new heart, 0 God, and put a steadfast spirit within us.
