# The hour has come

The Gospel (John 12 :20-33). Jesus often speaks, in St John's Gospel, of his 'hour', his supreme moment, when all that he has come to do on earth will be accomplished (in John 2 :4; 7 :30; 8 :20 -the 'hour' is not yet arrived; in John 13:1; 17 :1 he knows that it has come, at last). Twice, in today's Gospel, he speaks of this fateful 'hour' (vv. 23, 27). The time for his passion has arrived, and he both fears it yet longs for it. A momentary hesitation seems to cross his mind, so that his soul is troubled. Should he ask the Father to save him from the cross? No! He sets his mind to fulfil his task:
'For this purpose I have come unto this hour.' Further, Jesus states that in this hour of crisis and suffering he will be glorified.
Here, in the attitude of Jesus towards his coming passion, we find a perfect parallel to Heb. 5 :7-9. The high priest experiences the pain of obedience, yet offers himself up to it totally, and through it is glorified.
Around this central act of obedience, a fuller explanation is given about the relationship between death and life - between sacrificial death and fuller life. The first part of this explanation occurs, rather surprisingly, in vv. 24-27, where Our Lord has been told that some Greeks wish to speak with him. Instead of replying to their request, he states a fundamental principle to guide all his followers: 'unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone, but if it dies, it bears much fruit'. Following Christ involves despising one's earthly life and possessions, and being ready to yield up everything 'at the drop of a hat' for his service. The answer may not be quite the non sequitur it appears. Many Greeks, and people from every other nation, wish to see their saviour. They would have no opportunity to do so, unless that saviour should first die, and then he glorified. Perhaps St John saw this connection between the stranger's desire to see Christ, and the saying about the seed of wheat that dies in order to live.
Jesus explains more fully in w. 32-33 the relation between his sacrificial death, and the life of men. In his own person, he exemplifies the general principle: life, through death. 'If I be lifted up from the earth' he says 'I will draw all men to myself.' This 'lifting-up' means, in the language of John's Gospel, two things. It is both the physical posture of crucifixion (cf. John 3 :14 - 'as Moses lifted up the bronze serpent in the desert ...') and the divine act which Jesus was glorified, on account of his obedient death. Just as he is lifted up in torment by sinful men, even so he is raised in immortal triumph, by the Father, to be the saviour of all. And so, through his death and glory, he will draw men to himself, to give them faith and life and forgiveness.
The 'hour' is the moment when God receives from Jesus (high priest) the supreme tribute of obedient sacrifice; when Jesus is given the crown of glory by the Father; and when all sinful men and women can come to receive forgiveness of their sins. It is indeed the time spoken about by Jeremiah, when a new covenant is offered to us, and the law of Christ is impressed upon our hearts.
