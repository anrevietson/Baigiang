# The new covenant

First Reading (Jer. 31 :31-34). Our first reading today is among the greatest texts in the Old Testament. The prophet Jeremiah stands at a cross-roads - a real turning-point - in salvation history. He surveys God's dealings with Israel over the centuries, and compares what has gone before with what now lies in store. The pivotal word on which all revolves is 'covenant'. Looking back to the heroic days of the exodus from Egypt, God points to the covenant he made then with his people (through Moses); it was a solemn agreement of friendship, under which that privileged people would follow the law of their God, and be blessed in return with peace and prosperity. Yet this covenant, for all its goodness, has not been a success. It has failed, because the people had not the fidelity or spirit to keep their side of it. And so, still wanting his people to be saved, God promised a new state of affairs, a revised covenant which will help men more effectively to live the law of holiness.
Jeremiah proclaimed this merciful word of God at a time when Israel had all but disappeared, during the Babylonian exile of 586-536 BC. Contrary to the facile guarantees of the false prophets, the holy city of Jerusalem was captured and destroyed; the liturgy of the temple had been terminated; all of Israel's hopes were crushed, by the deportation of her best men and women to far-off Babylon. Such a situation fulfilled the grim threats that are found in Deut. 4 :23-28 and 28 :l5-68, where Israel is warned that her very survival depends upon remaining loyal to Yahweh and to his precepts. Indeed, the title 'Deuteronomic History' is given to the books (Joshua to 2 Kings) which recount the details of Israel's story in the light of the promises and threats contained in Deuteronomy. It is depressing to read (in 2 Kings) how consistently faithless were the chosen people and their kings. For their prolonged ignoring of their side of the covenant, God eventually sends the awful chastisement of complete defeat, of bereavement and of exile. Sinking into oblivion Israel receives new hope through Jeremiah; he says in God's name: I will restore you again, better than you were before. (This is the burden of chapters 30-31.) Of this section of his prophecy, today's four verses are the highlight; they condense his whole message, and are rightly called Jeremiah's 'spiritual testament'.
In the final time, 'after these days', God will admit all of his people (houses of Israel and Juda) into a new covenant of love and loyalty. This bond will surpass the 'Sinai covenant' in that the people's inner hearts will be involved; they will be swept along by a great spirit of inward desire, to keep the law of God. He himself will be their teacher, personally drawing out the faithful devotion of each. This new intimacy pervades the phrase (so beloved of Jeremiah, cf. 7 :23; 11 :4; 24 :7; 30 :22; 32 :38) 'I will be their God, and they shall be my people.' A long time was to elapse, before Jeremiah's 'Testament' would be fulfilled. Not until Jesus came, was the covenant written deeply on to the hearts of men, and only in him has it come to completion. Our understanding the Last Supper and of the cross is helped immensely, when we see in Christ's saving sacrifice the realisation of what is promised in Jer. 31, a new covenant written by God upon the hearts of men.
