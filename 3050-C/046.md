# I am doing a new thing

First Reading (Is. 43:16-21). Deutero-Isaiah, that anonymous inspired poet and prophet who has given us chapters forty to fifty-five of the book of Isaiah, tells his people time and again to look back on the evidences of divine power, love and fidelity to be seen in the creation of the world and in the history of Israel. He can point in particular to the exodus from Egypt, which is really the beginning of Israel's history as a people and which with the pass-over stood at the very centre of her religious life. These events of the past were no mere empty facts, past and gone in their occurrence. Rather were they evidence of the activity of a living God, of the God of Israel, bound to his people by his covenant and ever actively present in her history, leading her on to the goal set her. When Deutero-Isaiah addresses the Jews they are in exile in Babylon, far from the promised land. His message to them is that there will be a new exodus, a redemption so glorious that the great events of the past need scarcely be remembered any longer. Their God is the redeemer who will gather together the scattered exiles and lead them back to their homeland. Today's reading begins by recalling that it was God who led Israel through the Red Sea at the exodus from Egypt. He then destroyed Egypt's chariots and armies. They were 'snuffed out, never to rise again' (cf. Exod. 15:4-5). The marvels of the exodus period will now be repeated, but this time in the desert between Babylon and Palestine. Israel can see the past being repeated in the present and look forward to a repetition of it in the future because her God is the God of the covenant. The exodus and covenant could never merely be events of the past for the simple reason that in each of them God's fidelity to his promise was involved. The first exodus fulfilled the promise made to Abraham, and was at the same time a divine pledge that the divine plan of redemption would be perfected. And so Israel looked forward to a new exodus and a new covenant. These came long after the days of Deutero-Isaiah, with the death and resurrection of Christ in fact. With this Old Testament reading the liturgy thus takes us to the threshold of the New, to the new and eternal covenant in the blood of Christ.
