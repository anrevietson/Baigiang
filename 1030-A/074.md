# Two questions about John

Gospel (Mt 11:2-11). Today's Gospel falls naturally into two sections, each concerned with a question: Who is Jesus? Who is John the Baptist? The two parts are united by the theme that the promise is now fulfilled. The one who was to come is now here. The last days have dawned.
John sends to ask Jesus who he is. The question seems strange: in the Gospel of Matthew, John has already had an opportunity to meet and see Jesus for himself. He has acknowledged the greatness of Jesus. Perhaps Matthew wished to remind us that we have to ask over and over again, the question about how Jesus is at the centre of our lives. John may have been genuinely puzzled by the reports he had of the words and actions of Jesus. His earlier meeting with Jesus may not have been as conclusive for him as the apostles later considered it to be. On hearing that Jesus was preaching, he did not feel that he was threatened, but asked if Jesus indeed was the one for whom the people were waiting.
As elsewhere in the Gospel, Jesus gives an important statement in reply to a question that could seem naive or even sarcastic. This reply summarises all that we have been told about him in the previous chapters. Jesus interprets what he has been doing and saying. He uses phrases and turns of speech from the prophet Isaiah. Jesus is saying: see what I have done. Remember what has been promised. He implied that we should draw our own conclusions.
The statements Jesus makes about John are very interesting. He asks what notions we had about John? The image of the reed 'swaying in the wind' can be taken in various ways. It can be seen as an ordinary event, or a reference to a weak, pliant, flattering spirit. It can even be seen as a spectacular miracle if the reeds are a reference to the Exodus, when the sea was driven back for the Jews. Did people expect John to be a splendidly dressed Messiah? Jesus is clear. If he was wearing fine clothes, his place was in a palace!
In fact John usually wore camel hair and leather belt, and though he was now in a palace, he was in a prison of that palace. Yet he is the greatest of the children of women, for not only is he a great prophet, but he himself is a fulfilment of prophecy. John is one of the figures of the last age. John's greatness is pointed out, and then the greatness of those in the kingdom is left to be supposed, for it is greater than this.
So we are given a solution to the problem posed by the slight element of competition there seems to have been between the followers of John the Baptist and the followers of Jesus of Nazareth.
