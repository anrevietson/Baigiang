# The fall

First Reading (Gen. 3:9-15). After the man and woman had eaten the fruit of the tree, they recognize that they are naked, and what that implies. They realize that they have sinned, are ashamed in each other's presence, and know that their intimate relationship with God in their state of innocence has broken down. They hide from him but cannot escape his voice. The good psychology which we have met earlier in the description of the sin is continued in the account of the confession. Each strives to put the blame on someone else. The man accuses the woman. The woman points to the serpent.
The sentences of Yahweh appear in rhyme because of their importance. There is a twofold punishment in each case, that inflicted by God, and that imposed by his victim. We should also remark that the serpent alone is cursed by God, but not the man or the woman. We read today the punishment of the serpent.
Crawling on one's belly signifies the most wretched form of life. It was a punishment reserved for the adversaries of the Lord or his chosen ones. Thus Mic. 7:17; Is. 49:23, and Ps. 72:9 concerning the anointed king: 'May his foes bow down before him, and his enemies lick the dust!' The repulsive element in the brute creation is regarded as coming somehow from the entry of sin in the world. It is suggested that this may disappear with the revelation of the glory in Rom. 8:18-25. The real tension, however, exists between God and the evil forces in the world which lead man away from his creator.
Secondly, the serpent suffers at the hands of his victim, the woman. Since the serpent has seduced her, enmity arises between them. It will continue between the seed of the woman (mankind), and the seed of the serpent (the evil power in our world). This will last until the seed of the woman shall have crushed the head of the serpent, whereas the serpent will try to snatch at (or lie in wait for) his heel. [The masculine pronoun usage is not simply a reference to the Hebrew zera ('seed'), but to an individual who will oppose the serpent. I have suggested a variation between the action of both, based on the possibility of a difference in the verbs shuf and shafaf ('snatch at' - 'lie in wait for'). Such a translation would be important, as it accentuates the idea of victory over evil, rather than that of continuous strife. We could then speak of the Yah-wist's optimism.]
The passage, of course, provides the background for a Gospel fulfilment in which we find Jesus contrasting himself to the prince of demons, against his opponents' accusations.
The Responsorial Psalm (Ps. 129) takes up the theme of God's mercy, who forgives men their sins. They could not stand up again without his saving help. The temple watchmen announced the time for morning sacrifice at daybreak. We wait, still in some obscurity, for the second coming. We hope in the Lord. We can now carry this idea into the next reading.
