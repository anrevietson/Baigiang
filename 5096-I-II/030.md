# Bà góa nghèo trao hiến tất cả, vượt lên lòng đạo phô trương

The first, vv. 38 ff., contains a very severe invective against the scribes: how they distinguished themselves from the ordinary people by their pretentious and pompous dress, their desire for honour in the market-place, in synagogues, and at feasts, and in their abuse of the generous religious dispositions of widows to their own financial advantage. The second section, vv. 41-44, provides a very striking contrast: Jesus commends the example of the poor widow who contributed all that she had to the temple offerings. Perhaps it was Mark's intention in placing these two sections side by side to highlight this contrast, and to bring to a climax his account of Jesus' growing disillusionment with the religious leaders his conflict with the chief priests, scribes and elders (11:27-33), with the Pharisees and Herodians (12 :13-17) and with the Sadducees (12 :18-27) leading up to the severe denunciation of the scribes in today's passage (which is seen in even sharper relief by the tender remarks about the widow) . The liturgists suggest that the anti-scribe invective may be omitted from today's Gospel reading, presumably on the grounds that it might distract from the common theme of the first and third readings (the selfless giving of the two widows). On the other hand some zealous souls may be moved to include it as a preliminary to inveighing against ostentatious clerical garb, Or long-winded spontaneous prayers, or even the exploitation of women in the service of religion! More seriously, the reading of the two sections together will bring into sharper focus the contrast between the sham religion of the professional and the unquestioning generosity of the simple faithful. The account of the widow's two mites is a typical pronouncement story, where the story is told, not for its own intrinsic interest, but because the incident provided the occasion for Jesus' teaching on self-emptying (cf. Phil. 2 :7). Some doubt the historicity of the event on the grounds that Jesus could scarcely have known that her gift represented the widow's entire livelihood, and also because there are parallels to it in Jewish tradition. Those commentators suggest that Jesus took over a Jewish parable and used it in his own teaching - at a later date tradition read the incident back into Jesus' life as an incident which he himself witnessed. However, although this reconstruction is a possible one, the arguments on which it depends are weak, and do not convincingly rule out the genuineness of the story. Jn any case, even the most esoteric exegesis cannot hide the central message : Jesus commends the widow, not because of the amount of her contribution, but because of the sacrifice she made to give it. This example of the widow is an embarrassment to the most generous of us: our contributions, both on a national and personal level, come from our superfluity, and it is only when we have given everything that we will emulate the widow. This story of the widow's selfless giving breaks the rhythm of Mark's Gospel plan: he inserts it, almost as an appendix, after the conflicts with the Jewish authorities. But it is surely highly significant that both Mark and Luke place the incident so close to the account of the Passion of Jesus. Jesus did not merely commend the generosity of the widow - he surpassed it in emptying himself, not merely of his possessions, but of his very life, 'yes, even to death on a cross' (Phil. 2 :8).
