# God's hidden wisdom

Second Reading (1 Cor. 2 :6-10). In last Sunday's second reading Paul stressed the fact that Christianity does not depend on human philosophy or wisdom. God chose what was foolish by human reckoning to shame the wise (1 Cor. 1:27). He destroyed the wisdom]n of the wise and brought to nothing the learning of the learned (I Cor 1:19). What these texts mean is that God did not use human wisdom to save man; this he did through the cross which is apparently sheer foolishness according to a natural way of looking at things. What God rejected, and rejects, is that form of wisdom which makes man's mind the measure of reality. St Paul is no enemy of reason or of theology. He himself, in fact, is the greatest of New Testament theologians. He grappled with problems as they arose and solved them in the light of revealed truth, and in so doing developed the teaching of the faith that had been handed down to him. Soon after writing this present letter he would have to address himself to the problem of the relationship of the external norm of' law and grace in justification. Later still the role of Christ in creation would have to be set forth clearly, this to counter false preachings that were to arise.
Nor is Paul in any way in favour of ignorance in Christians. Quite the contrary. He wants them to be well grounded in their faith and to come to an ever deeper understanding of it. St. Paul would be no enemy of theology, were he alive today. In fact he would be its staunchest defender, as theology, i.e. the explanation of revealed truth, is a necessity not a luxury, for the Church.
This deeper understanding of the faith is what in today's reading Paul calls wisdom. It is basically an understanding of God's plan of salvation, a plan that remained a secret in God's mind to be revealed through Christ and his Church, revealed through the work of the Holy Spirit, giving believers a deeper insight into the content of revealed truth and indicating its bearing on new problems arising within the Church. But this wisdom is far removed from that other of which Paul spoke, that 'wisdom' which does not draw enlightenment from revelation. It is not a wisdom arising from man's mind without revelation nor is it understood, Paul says, 'by the masters of our age', i.e. by the pagan rulers or wise men of his own day, or possibly by the angels who were believed to govern human actions.
Paul further says that the wisdom of which he speaks is reserved for the 'mature', i.e. for believers already grounded in the Christian faith. Paul was a realist in his manner of teaching. First of all believers had to be taught the essentials of the faith; this then could be developed, deepened and further explained, when the believers had grasped the essentials. Paul believed in the maxim that one should not attempt to fly before having first learned to walk. Theology without a thorough grasp of the catechism, of the essentials, can lead to confusion and disaster.
Nor is Paul thinking of arid abstraction when, he speaks of 'wisdom'. Wisdom for him is a deep insight into the mystery of Christ, into his person and teaching and the bearing of both on Christian life. And for him this insight comes through revelation, not necessarily extraordinary revelations but through enlightenment of the Holy Spirit, who alone can teach the true meaning of Christianity. Hence, prayer is required with reflection.
This present reading has some evident lessons for us. We should strive to come to an ever deeper knowledge of divine revelation, which is God's revealed wisdom. But we should also realise that to understand Christ's religion and Church as we ought we should pray to the Holy Spirit for enlightenment.
