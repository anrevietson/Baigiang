# The new righteousness

The Gospel (Mat. 5 :17-37). Despite certain obscure and difficult expressions, the central message of the reading is clear. The commandments and the central message of the Pentateuch (Moses, the law) and the prophets still remain very much a reality. They did not end with the coming of Christ. Jesus came to fulfil them, to bring them to perfection, not to abolish them. The coming of Christ, however, did mean a change, a very real and radical change. While the commandments remain they are given a deeper meaning by Jesus. Their essential message is retained but reinterpreted in the light of the new age introduced by Jesus. Jesus came to inaugurate the new covenant, a covenant to be written in man's heats, in the innermost part of his being. He came to transform man from within, to give him a new heart, a renewed inner being. One way in which Jesus does this is illustrated by his reinterpretation of the fifth, sixth and eight commandments (in the Roman Catholic numbering of the commandments).
The fifth commandment forbids us to kill another person. It forbids a specific action and is thus limited in its object. Murder, however, is but the outcome of envy, hatred, etc., nourished in the heart. In Jewish eyes the extreme malice of murder lay in the fact that it destroyed the image of God in man. Christ so reinterprets the fifth commandment as to have it embrace all those feelings and emotions of which murder is but the outcome. All hatred is to be banished from the heart, and kindness, not revenge, should repay hatred and insult. Man is God's image and is worthy of our respect. Consequently, insults and taunts of any sort are forbidden.
Adultery, too, is born in the heart. And so the sixth commandment forbids not merely the act itself, but likewise all thoughts and desires that lead to it.
The eighth commandment forbids false oaths. In Palestine of Christ's day oaths and vows of all kinds were being taken. The Jews swore by all kinds of formulae, by heaven, by earth, by the temple, etc., generally avoiding direct mention of the divine name. Jesus tells his followers not to take any oath; the sole word of a follower of Christ should be his bond. Simplicity of word and action should be the rule. When he means yes, let him simply say yes; when he means no, let him say no. Confirming his word by an oath, calling on God as witness, is not what Christ wants. St John Chrysostom believed that these words of Christ forbid Christians to take any oath. Since normal Christian practice is in point of fact far removed from the Gospel ideal, both Church and State have believed otherwise. And yet Christ's words can only condemn the need for oath-taking. It is also rather anomalous to swear a solemn oath to God on the Holy Bible which contains Christ's word telling us not to swear at all!
The present-day emphasis on love as the motive power in Christian life may lead some to forget or play down the reality of the commandments, of the traditional ten and many more besides. We all know that the ten commandments - in fact all divine commands can be reduced to two: love of God and love of the neighbour. This is so because the first group treats of man's relation to God, the second of his relationship to his fellow-men. We can also say that all the commandments can be reduced to the single commandment of love. It is equally true, however, that this one commandment or these two contain all ten! Love is genuine only when it keeps all God's commands. 'If you love me keep my commandments' are Christ's own words. Without God's objective law as its norm love is hut an emotion.
There is no conflict between love and law for a Christian. Nor does fulfilment of the commandments mean formalistic religion For a follower of Christ the commandments are much more 'You shall' than 'You shall not'. They are commandments rather than prohibitions! 'You shall keep alive and respect your fellow-man' rather than 'You shall not kill'. 'You shall be truthful' rather than you shall not give false witness'. The commandments are not restricted in the object to the minimum required for salvation or the well-being of society. Through love their object is unlimited.
