# The baptism of Cornelius

First Reading (Acts 10 :25-26, 34-35, 44-48). Today we read of the conversion and baptism of the pagan Cornelius and his household. It was a turning point in the history of the early Church. From then on it was clear that the Church would be universal and it began to spread among the Gentiles, outside Palestine. In Cornelius we have another example of that faith of the pagans praised by Jesus. The humility of Cornelius, too, contrasts with the pride of the Pharisees and other Jews who boasted in their privileges. The prostration expressed great reverence and respect -a reverence given to divinity rather than humanity. Hence Peter's reaction. Peter's speech here is notable for its full description of the earthly ministry of Jesus and for its lack of an exhortation to repentance and the slightness of its allusion to scripture.
The inclusion of Gentiles in the Church made Peter (and the other apostles) aware of God's love for all men and his will to save all men. The idea that God has no favourites but judges everyone according to one's fidelity. is found several times in the teaching of Peter and of Paul. This does not mean that Israel was not a chosen people but mere belonging to the chosen people did not guarantee salvation, nor does mere belonging to the Church in name guarantee salvation. We must be not hearers of the word only but doers as well. The language of sacrifice used in v. 35 ('is acceptable to him') reminds us of the prophecy of Is. 56 :7 that when the fullness of time came the pagans' sacrifices would be acceptable to God (cf. Acts 10 :31)
The fact that the Holy Spirit was poured out on his listeners just as it had been on the apostles at Pentecost impressed Peter very much. . It showed God's approval. Peter refers to it and draws the comparison when defending his action (Acts 11:17). This is the 'Pentecost of the pagans'. As at Pentecost, so now, those who have received the Spirit speak about the marvels of God in a strange way. In 1 Cor. 12 :10 and 14 :4 the gift of tongues seems to be ecstatic cries of praise unintelligible to the listeners. This is probably true here as well, although in Acts 2 .4-11 there seems to be
reference to foreign languages, each of the foreigners present under-standing his own language. (The account there, however, may be symbolic.) The conferring of baptism after receiving the Spirit is not typical. This is a special occasion. What is important is that baptism was regarded as the necessary form of admission into the Church. Saul, too, was baptised after his conversion (Acts 9 :19). Usually it was not the apostles themselves but some of their helpers who actually administered baptism (cf. Acts 19 :5). Converts were baptised in the name of Jesus Christ. It seems that at this early stage the Trinitarian formula was not yet used. It is interesting to note that the Jewish Christians in Jerusalem were more disturbed by Peter's lodging with the uncircumcised an they were with his authorisation of baptism for the Gentiles (Acts 11 :23; cf. Gal; 2 :11 )
The Responsorial Psalm (Ps. 97.1-4) calls on all men to rejoice in God's saving justice which is no longer confined to any one group or nation.
