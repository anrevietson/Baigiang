# Comfort my people

First Reading (Is. 40:1-5, 9-11). The fall of Jerusalem in 587 BC spelled the end of the kingdom of Judah and with it the rule of the David dynasty. To human reckoning all seemed lost. The cream of the nation was in exile in Babylon. What was now left was but the remnant of a nation, a people at the mercy of a powerful empire. To the eyes of faith things looked much different. For the believer the exile could only be temporary, a punishment, a period of hard labour with which to atone for sins of rebellion committed against the God of Israel. The prophet Jeremiah, who had tried to avert the disaster by calling on Israel to turn away from sin, reminded God's people that the exile in Babylon was to be for a limited period - seventy years, which for the prophet was a round number. Its end was determined by God who would also break the pride of Babylon. After this period the exiles would return rejoicing across the intervening desert to their homeland of Judah and to the holy city of Zion. This homecoming would be a second exodus. Jeremiah's words generated hope in the exiles' hearts. Then as the decades moved by an anonymous poet-prophet arose among the Jews in exile to comfort and encourage them. This is the anonymous author who has given us chapters forty to fifty-five of the book of Isaiah, a work rightly known as 'The Book of Consolation'. God, this prophet told them, was about to intervene to lead his people, like a Shepherd leads his flock, back across the desert to Zion. Theirs would be a pleasant homecoming across the wilderness. A new highway would be built to make it easy. In his poetic vision the prophet hears the voice of God commanding a crier run speedily to Jerusalem (or commanding Jerusalem herself, either understanding is possible) to bear the good tidings, the gospel. (It is from the book of Isaiah that the New Testament has got the word for 'gospel', 'godspell',) 'good spell'.) The gospel is none other than the good news of her redemption, of Yahweh's intervention to save his people and her. The poet himself urges the crier, the precursor or forerunner, to declare the good news openly in the towns of Judah. His message is to be: 'Here is your God.' So runs the biblical text. By New Testament times this was paraphrased in the synagogues of Palestine for the ordinary people as: 'The kingdom of your God is revealed.' The glory of the Lord and the coming of God's kingdom would be seen in his intervention to save his people. The actual return from the exile in Babylon, that took place in 537 and later, fell far short of what was promised in the poet's vision. Later generations of Jews had patiently to await the fulfilment of God's word with the advent of the second exodus of salvation. In the intervening centuries some elements ~f the prophet's message became allegorised. The highway to be made straight came to be looked on as man's way of life, his moral behaviour. God's kingdom was to be prepared for by repentance. None the less it was still believed that the beginning of the messianic era would be associated with the desert. It was there, as at the first exodus, that Israel would meet her God. Thus did the biblical text and the later understanding of it prepare for the fulfilment that came in the person of Jesus and was ushered in by the Baptist.
