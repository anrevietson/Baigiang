# Comfort my people

First Reading (Is 40:1-5.9-11). Historically speaking, the end of the Israel's exile was due to shift in international power politics brought about by the rise of Cyrus, a Persian prince who came to power in Babylon in 539. The new regime, unlike the one it replaced, no longer needed to hold the hereditary aristocracy of its conquered territories in the security of exile. So a year later, Cyrus permitted the return of the Jews to their ancestral land. The latter part of the Book of Isaiah, which begins with this prophetic oracle of comfort, interprets this historical event in the light of Israel's belief in Yahweh as the Lord of history. Not only is Cyrus seen as God's servant later in the book, but the decree of return is seen here as promulgated, not in the council chambers of Babylon, but in heavenly council. There is an excited quality about this opening oracle which makes it rather difficult to distinguish the different speakers but leaves us in no doubt about the hope now pinned on this event as a new beginning. The opening words are certainly God's message of comfort, now that the lesson of sinfulness has been absorbed in the sorrow of exile, the end of servitude can be announced. A mysterious voice (either in heaven itself or from an earthly prophet, is unclear) proclaims the preparations necessary; just as the ancient royal progress called for the rebuilding and repair of the sacred way, so the return is seen as a magnificent procession in which the Lord's glory will be revealed to all people. A prophet is instructed to announce the glad tidings of Yahweh's arrival to the disinherited poor who were left behind in the land. Two images of God as conquering hero and gentle shepherd of Israel come together in the concluding verses. Even if, historically speaking, the decree of Cyrus lead to a gradual return of small groups, we catch the echo of the Exodus, foundational event of Israel's past.
