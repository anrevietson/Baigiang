# The first prophecy of the passion

The Gospel (Mat. 16:21-27). In Gospel tradition the first prophecy of the passion follows the confession of St Peter. In St Matthew's Gospel the promise to Peter gives the verses which follow even greater poignancy.
The opening words of the reading indicate that Jesus' prophecies of his suffering to come were a continuing thing. He did begin to make it clear immediately after he had elicited the first clear expression of the disciples' faith (mouthed by Peter), but the evangelist's insertion of a time-lag helps to temper the sharpness of the rebuke to Peter as well as to enhance the continuing teaching on the true meaning of discipleship, the following of a crucified Lord.
Now that Jesus and the faith of his disciples has centred the reality of the Kingdom on his own person, the fate of his person is crucial for the existence of the Kingdom, and will in turn deeply touch the faith of his followers. Like the other evangelists, Matthew is concerned in this passage with discipleship, rather than the foresight of Jesus. The argument seems less insistent on the final fate, since Jesus is to be raised up on the third day', than on the fact that this is but the path to the resurrection. Peter's refusal to accept this path at once withdraws him from his God-given faith; he stands across the way to the cross, and thus embodies the adversary of God. The only way that Peter's faith may gain its power is for the apostle to fall in behind and tread the same path.
Said concretely to Peter, the evangelist has our Lord broaden out a particular occurrence to apply to all true discipleship of Christ. This following behind through suffering to the resurrection is not optional - it is a matter of life and death. To accept is to be endowed with the faith of Peter; to refuse is to obstruct God's path as Peter tries to do.
To believe is to fall in behind our Lord. To live for God is to trace in one's own life the life of Christ. The cross is actual and symbolic: actual because it stood on Calvary, symbolic because it represents the sufferings, persecutions, martyrdoms, indifference, moral Struggles, lovelessness which every follower of Christ is bound to meet. Jeremiah is not alone in feeling the oppression and constraint of God's call. Every disciple of Christ has in his own way to face it.
Second Reading (Rom. 12:1-2). The hymn to God's wisdom and mercy, indeed the whole of chapters 9-11, in which the mercy of God to all men is extolled, form a preparation for the second, exhortatory section of Romans.
As the first section of the letter has taught us to see the Holy Spirit as God's permanent gift of salvation, so Paul would have us live according to the Spirit and not according to the flesh. The verses of this reading form an exhortation to this mentality. Christian striving is less an effort to become fully ourselves at some future date, than to express in our outward style of life the spirit-endowed being which we have already become. The Christian offers worship to God in the manner worthy of his renewed being by embracing a Christ-like life. Just as Christ in his day-to-day living sacrificed himself for others, up until the supreme sacrifice of the cross, so his disciples must follow this same sacrificial path of offering all we do to God. This will truly make us human as well as Christian, though in Paul the distinction may have little validity.
In the chapters which follow, in general and in particular, Paul will consider the workings of the Holy Spirit in our Christian action. The Holy Spirit has formed in us a new mind. The apostle has spoken of this before: 'The Spirit too comes to help us in our weakness. For when we cannot choose words in order to pray properly, the Spirit himself expresses our plea in a way that could never be put into words, and God who knows everything in our hearts knows perfectly well what he means, and that the pleas of the Saints expressed by the Spirit are according to the mind of God.' Clearly Paul is thinking of these words in the present passage of Romans. Our knowing of God's mind and will depend upon the action of the Holy Spirit. The Spirit is not helping us out when we become weak, for our weakness without him as gift is the weakness that Paul earlier described. He brings us the ability as his aid. As his activity welds us to the mind of Christ, our activity becomes the sacrifice of our whole being to God and to our human brothers.
The perspectives of today's readings converge on the vocation every Christian has to re-present the whole work of Christ. We live Christ's life, and should never forget that this life is also a concrete earthly life made life-giving Spirit by his resurrection. The servant is like his master, and our Way to the resurrection is along the same road.
