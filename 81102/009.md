# The wedding at Cana

The Gospel (John 2:1-11). The semi-continuous reading of the gospels for the Sundays of the year begins next Sunday. In today's reading the Church still looks back to the beginnings of Christ's ministry as narrated by John. The narrative of the miracle at Cana read in this year's cycle links this Sunday with the feast of the Epiphany and with the Sunday following on it (i.e. the feast of Christ's baptism). All three recount manifestations (epheiphanies) of Christ's glory.. Ass is the case with many of Christ's miracles recounted by John (who calls them 'signs'), we must consider the Cana episode at two levels a: what actually occurred, and the deeper spiritual meaning of the event. Weddings were joyful events in the east, the festivities continuing over a number of days. From the presence of Mary, it would appear that. she was. a relative or close friend of the newly-wedded couple. Her delicacy had her realise that the shortage a of wine would bring embarrassment to them. Jesus' reply to her request reads literally : 'Woman, what to me and to you?' 'Woman ' was then an accepted manner by which to address females (cf. 4:11) and corresponded more or less to our ' madam'. 'What to me and to you' (a phrase used both in the Old and New Testaments : Judges 11:12, etc.; Mark 1:24; 5:7) signifies disassociation of some land. Jesus, while son of Maryn, is also Son of his heavenly Father who has given him a divine mission to fulfil. As such he necessarily stands apart from all humans. His reply here is thus somewhat similar to that in the temple (Luke 2:49). The 'hour' of Jesus in John's gospel is above all that of his death and resurrection ; his 'exaltation' as John calls it. Here it may also embrace the hour the Father has willed that he show his glory through miracles. While it is difficult to find an exact rendering for the phrase in the present passage, no absolute refusal is intended. And the water is changed into the host of wine due to Mary's intervention. By this miracle Jesus manifested his glory, the glory that was his from eternity as the only Son of the Father (1:14). This glory, hidden through the incarnation, shone through his human nature on such occasions as the transfiguration. To the eye of faith it was always visible and seen at work in his miracles, which through faith become signs of the power of Jesus and of the nature of his teaching. It was in this sense that his disciples saw his glory at Cana, and seeing it had their faith in Jesus increased. It is significant that at Cana Jesus manifested his divine glory through an act performed from a humanitarian consideration, and this at his mother's request. God's glory is manifested through love and concern for others.
John calls Jesus' miracle at Cana a sign. What occurred there is a symbol of higher truths. The real bride and
Groom the wedding was Jesus (cf. 3:25). The miracle he performed symbolised a greater work of his, namely the transformation of the water of Jewish religion int6 the strong wine of Christianity. In the opening section of his Gospel John speaks of a new beginning, a new creation ('In the beginning . . .'). For the Jews water symbolised their law with its reputed purifying effects. The water jars at Cana symbolised their religion and teaching. This was changed by Jesus into new wine (cf. Mark 2:22), the wine proper to the new age when the wedding-feast of the lamb is celebrated. At Cana, however, this real change was on1y foreshadowed. Jesus' hour had not yet come. What Cana symbolised became a reality with Calvary and all it signified. The Spirit was then given and the new age was inaugurated. As at Cana, so also on Calvary Mary was present and addressed as 'Woman' (19:27). At the beginning of humanity there also was the 'woman' Eve (Gen. 3:15), mother of all the living . . . And on Calvary Mary is given to believers, represented by John, as their mother.
