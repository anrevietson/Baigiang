# The Book of Consolation

First Reading (Jer. 31:7-9). This passage belongs to that part of the book of Jeremiah which is called 'The Book of Consolation' embracing chapters thirty-one to thirty-three. It is a collection of sayings attributed to Jeremiah and addressed to a beaten people in need of words of hope and encouragement. There is much disagreement about the precise historical situation in which the oracles were born. Many authors place them in the period of the Babylonian exile, and in this case they have come down to us through the mediation of someone either quoting the very words of Jeremiah spoken on an earlier occasion, or giving his adaptation of those words. There is a growing weight of opinion which traces many of the oracles back to the earlier period of the preaching life of Jeremiah, in the reign of King Josiah (i.e., from the period of Jeremiah's call to prophetic ministry in 627 BC to the death of Josiah in 609 BC). In this case Jeremiah was not addressing himself to the inhabitants of the southern kingdom of Judah who were engaged in a process of religious reform under Josiah but to the survivors of the defeated northern kingdom of Israel who were demoralised under the yoke of Assyrian rule, and who had no hope of a return to the former glory which they had previously enjoyed. It is reckoned that there were about 70,000 of the inhabitants of the former kingdom still remaining in what was then the Assyrian province of Samaria, while their brothers were in exile in distant regions of the Assyrian empire. As well as living in subjugation to a foreign political power, the remnant' living in Samaria had to live side by side with the foreign elements introduced into the territory by the conquerors, and this had opened the way to apostasy in the worship of the foreign gods. In this period of his activity, then, Jeremiah preached a message of consolation, and a call to conversion to the real God. Even though they were beaten on all sides by their enemies. they would not be broken: 'With God on our side who can be against us?'
The particular form of the invitation to praise is similar to a particular genre of Hebrew hymn (called the 'imperative hymn'). It is possible that the text as we have it comes from a liturgical setting in which the worshippers were invited to sing the praises of Yahweh for his saving deeds of the past, and for the consoling assurance of a future salvation. It is also likely that this message of hope, spoken to the disillusioned inhabitants of the beaten northern kingdom, took on a renewed relevance for the exiles in Babylon after the collapse of the southern kingdom.
The Responsorial Psalm (Ps. 126); This is a liturgical song, probably written soon after the bitter experience of the Babylonian exile when the new sense of liberation was almost too much to be believed. (However, the Hebrew allows a translation which looks to a future salvation.) Even the nations praised (or will praise) Yahweh for his deliverance of his people; Israel, then, far from selfishly glorying in her election by Yahweh, rejoices that her salvation has redounded to his praise among the Gentiles. The memory of God's saving deeds in the past is the basis for the confident petition which now requests a deliverance from some present calamity. The final verse alludes to a future glory which will follow the trials of the present.
