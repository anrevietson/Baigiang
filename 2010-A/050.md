# The flight into Egypt

The Gospel (Mat. 2 :13-15, 19-23) tells us of a hardship endured by the Holy Family soon after the Magi had left. Judging from Herod's decision (after enquiry) to kill all the male children of two years or under, . we can conclude that the flight took place about two years after the birth of Jesus. Just as he had a dream regarding the virginal conception of Jesus, so now Joseph is informed in a dream of the danger. Through a dream, too, he is instructed . to return when the danger has passed. Dreams as a means of revelation are mentioned explicitly only by Matthew among New Testament authors (Acts mentions them, but as 'visions in the night'), but the Bible does not give them a prominent place as a medium of divine communication. They occur frequently in Rabbinic literature. The 'angel of the Lord' was, in early biblical texts, God himself, but later angels came to be seen as creatures and messengers of God. The destination of the Holy Family was not necessarily Egypt as we know it, but may have been Egyptian territory close to Palestine e.g. Gaza. Throughout his Gospel Matthew sees in the events of Christ's life the fulfilment of prophecy. This is especially true in the Infancy Narrative. It is not in the flight into Egypt but in the return that the prophecy is here seen fulfilled. The text from Hosea in its original setting refers to the call of Israel from Egypt at the Exodus. Jesus is presented as re-enacting in his own life the career of Israel; he ii the new Israel. When Herod died (4 BC) his kingdom was divided by the emperor Augustus among three of Herod's sons. Archelaus got Judea, Samaria, and Idumea; Herod Antipas got Galilee and Perea; Philip got territory east and north of Galilee. Archelaus's government was not successful and he was eventually deposed and exiled in AD 6. Luke tells us that Mary and Joseph were living in Galilee and went to Bethlehem. Matthew has not told us this and so Joseph's dream (v.22) explains why Jesus was brought up in Galilee and known as a Galilean. The citation in v.23 is found nowhere in the Old Testament. Possibly there is a word play on the Hebrew text of Is. 11:1 : 'A shoot springs from the stock of Jesse, a scion (neser) thrusts from his roots'. There is an Aramaic adjective, n6sraya, derived from the town-name 'Nazareth', and reading Is. 11 :1 Matthew would have seen the resemblance.
