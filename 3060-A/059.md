# The Passion according to Matthew

Gospel (Mt 26:14-27:66). The passion account in Matthew is consistent with Mark 14-i 6, although Matthew, written later, develops the themes and clearly shows these events as fulfilment of the Old Testament scriptures. The reading begins with the betrayal plans of Judas, one of the Twelve (Mt 26:14), recounts the meal at Passover time (26:17-25), the Eucharist (26:26-29), the foretelling of Peter's denial (26:49-56), the prayer in Gethsemane (26:57-68), Peter's denial (26:69-75), Jesus before Pilate, the trial and sentencing (27:1-26), the mockery (27:27-31), crucifixion, death and burial (27:32-61), and the guarding of the tomb (27:62-66)..
The passion narrative mentions Judas as one of the Twelve, and focuses on Peter as well. The Twelve are important to Matthew's understanding of discipleship and Church for they are central figures, models for other, and the foundation for the community. In the passion narrative, one of those closest to Jesus betrays him, Peter denies him, others fall asleep. and all, but for a few of the women, abandoned him in the end. The portrayal of Jesus is as King of the Jews during his trial and death, even though the soldiers use the title in a mocking way (Mt 27:11,28-30,42). The irony of the situation strikes the reader. When speaking of his betrayal, suffering and coming in glory, Jesus uses the title Son of Man, showing consistency in understanding between Matthew and Mark. (Mt 26:24,45,64). Throughout the narrative are reminders of Jesus' authority, "I tell you most solemnly," his self-possession before Pilate and in the face of death, and his awareness of what lies ahead as he predicts betrayal, denial, and loss of faith. Repeatedly, Matthew recalls that these events are in fulfilment of scripture, often quoting from the Old Testament or alluding to its content (Mt 26:24,54; 27:9,46). Jesus uses Psalm 22 in his prayer on the cross and his death results in dramatic signs as well as in the faith of a centurion, confessing Jesus as Son of God. The guarding of the tomb is unique to Matthew.
The richness and completeness of this account cannot he glossed over. However, two events deserve further attention because of their importance to Matthew's community and to our understanding of Jesus. These are Peter's denial and Jesus' prayer in the garden of Gethsemane.
The denial of Peter offers a stark contrast to his adamant protests that he will never lose faith. Jesus predicts that Peter will disown him three times. When this actually occurs, Peter responds to the inquiry of a servant-girl, then of another, and finally of bystanders. In seemingly insignificant situations, Peter denies Jesus, cursing and swearing that he does not know the man. Only in Matthew do we have Jesus' statements, "thou are Peter and upon this rock I will build my Church."(Mt 16:18) Peter's denial is a caution to the religious leaders of Matthew's community lest their confidence blind them to the reality that faith is tested in daily circumstances,.
